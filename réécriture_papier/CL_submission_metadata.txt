ABSTRACT
--------------------------------------------------------------------
Hallucination evaluation often classifies claims not entailed by reference
evidence as errors. This convention suits tasks with strict grounding but is incomplete
when a task permits bounded hypotheses or fictional invention. This position paper
argues that non-entailment alone should not determine a claim label. Evaluators
should instead use a task-specific truth contract with three fields: reference
evidence, permission scope, and required status marking. An entailed claim receives
the supported label, whereas a contradicted claim receives the hallucination label.
A canonical claim whose proposition is evidence-unknown receives licensed
divergence only when the proposition lies inside the permission scope and its
observed status marking satisfies the required status marking; otherwise it receives
the hallucination label. Coverage, adjudication, or contract-specification failures
suspend claim labeling rather than creating another label. A complete evaluation
also records requested and observed style-marking levels separately. The requested
level is available as context during claim recovery and supports a prompt--response
style-marking alignment check, but its effect on recovery remains an empirical
hypothesis and it never expands permission scope. We examine the distinction
through a purposive mapping of forty evaluation resources and five worked cases. Within the
selected sample, no resource jointly controls the three truth-contract fields and
claim-preserving style variation. The mapping is a diagnostic description of the
selected resources, not a prevalence estimate for the broader literature. The
proposal is unnecessary if a baseline combining factuality with
instruction-following evaluation yields equally reliable and informative
claim-level diagnoses.

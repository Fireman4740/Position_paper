\begin{abstract}
Hallucination evaluation often classifies claims not entailed by reference
evidence as errors.
This convention suits tasks with strict grounding but is incomplete when a task
permits bounded hypotheses or fictional invention.
This position paper argues that non-entailment alone should not determine a claim label.
Evaluators should instead use a task-specific \emph{truth contract} with three fields: reference evidence, permission scope, and required status marking.
An entailed claim receives the supported label, whereas a contradicted claim receives the hallucination label.
A canonical claim whose proposition is evidence-unknown receives licensed
divergence only when the proposition lies inside the permission scope and its
observed status marking satisfies the required status marking; otherwise it
receives the hallucination label.
Coverage, adjudication, or contract-specification failures suspend claim labeling rather than creating another label.
A complete evaluation also records requested and observed style-marking levels separately.
The requested level is available as context during claim recovery and supports a prompt--response style-marking alignment check, but its effect on recovery remains an empirical hypothesis and it never expands permission scope.
We examine the distinction through a purposive mapping of forty evaluation resources and five worked cases.
Within the selected sample, no resource jointly controls the three
truth-contract fields and claim-preserving style variation.
The mapping is a diagnostic description of the selected resources, not a prevalence estimate for the broader literature.
The proposal is unnecessary if a baseline combining factuality with
instruction-following evaluation yields equally reliable and informative
claim-level diagnoses.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Large language models (LLMs) can produce fluent claims that reference evidence contradicts or leaves unknown \citep{ji_survey_2023,huang_survey_2025}.
These errors can limit the use of LLMs in high-stakes settings.
Accordingly, research spans taxonomies, benchmarks, detection, and mitigation
\citep{ji_survey_2023,huang_survey_2025}.

An evidence-unknown claim is not always an error.
Fiction permits invention, whereas brainstorming can solicit novel suggestions
rather than reproduction of available evidence
\citep{franceschelli_creativity_2024,jiang_survey_2024,rick_supermind_2023}.
A hypothesis-generation task may likewise authorize explicitly marked,
evidence-unknown propositions.

A medical assistant should not present an unverified diagnosis as established.
A fiction-writing assistant must be able to invent characters and events.
The relevant distinction is therefore whether the task permits an
evidence-unknown claim, not merely whether the reference evidence entails it.

Current evaluation protocols do not always represent this distinction explicitly.
Factuality and source-faithfulness evaluations compare generated claims with
external or provided reference evidence
\citep{maynez_faithfulness_2020,kryscinski_evaluating_2020,min_factscore_2023}.
Creativity research commonly distinguishes novelty from usefulness or quality
\citep{runco_standard_2012,diedrich_are_2015};
CreativityPrism, for example, evaluates quality, novelty, and diversity
\citep{hou_creativityprism_2025}.
These dimensions do not by themselves determine whether a novel factual claim
violates a task constraint.
Consequently, two protocols can assign different labels to identical surface wording when their prompts establish different task conditions.

Claim recovery poses a separate problem when a response uses a marked style.
Stylistic foregrounding makes linguistic form salient through devices such as deviation, parallelism, metaphor, and unusual phrasing \citep{leech_short_style_1981,vanpeer_stylistics_2020}.
Text style-transfer work commonly treats content preservation as a separate
objective from changing stylistic form
\citep{jin_deep_2022,rao_dear_2018,briakou_evaluating_2021}.
A procedure for claim recovery may nevertheless literalize a metaphor, add a spurious claim, or omit the intended one.
Whether evaluators preserve claims under claim-preserving style variation is an empirical question.
Protocols that do not expose style-marking level as a controlled variable
cannot measure this possible error.

\paragraph{Our position}
We argue that non-entailment is an evidence result, not a complete claim label.
A claim should be evaluated under the task's truth contract.
A \emph{claim} is a truth-conditional commitment attributed to the response: something the response presents as being the case and that can therefore be checked against evidence.
A \emph{response span} $s$ is a text segment from which an evaluator recovers one or more claims.
We use $x$ for the literal prompt and $p$ for the complete task context, including applicable system instructions, the user's request, dialogue history, domain constraints, and attached evidence.
We write the contract as $\TC(p)=(O_p,\Gamma_p,\mu_p)$.
The reference evidence $O_p$ specifies the standard of support.
The permission scope $\Gamma_p$ specifies which evidence-unknown content the task authorizes, and the required status marking $\mu_p$ specifies how that content must be presented.
Requested style-marking level $\sigma_p$ is a separate task variable.
It is available as contextual input during claim recovery and is compared with the observed style-marking level $\widehat{\sigma}(y,p)$ in a separate prompt--response style-marking alignment check.
An entailed claim receives \SUP\ (supported).
A contradicted claim receives \Hall\ (hallucination) with reason
\textsc{contradicted}.
A canonical claim whose proposition $q^*$ is evidence-unknown receives \LD\
(licensed divergence) only when $q^*$ lies inside $\Gamma_p$ and its observed
status marking $\rho^*$ satisfies $\mu_p$; otherwise it receives \Hall\ with
reason \textsc{out-of-scope} or \textsc{marking-failure}.
These labels apply to canonical claims.
A complete response is represented by the structured response-level report
$R(y\mid p)$,
which keeps the claim trace and separate diagnostics visible.
The contract does not change what is true.
It determines how evidence state, permission scope, and required status marking
compose into a claim label.

\paragraph{Why a structured contract rather than factuality plus instruction-following evaluation?}
Evidence-based factuality and source-faithfulness methods ask whether a claim is supported by a designated external reference or input source \citep{maynez_faithfulness_2020,min_factscore_2023}.
Instruction-following evaluation instead asks whether a response satisfies the constraints expressed by the task \citep{zhou_ifeval_2023}; intent-aware evaluation can further diagnose omitted or misinterpreted query conditions \citep{hao_faithqa_2025}.
These objectives are complementary, and work that studies them jointly shows that instruction following and evidential faithfulness may require explicit reconciliation \citep{wu_dancing_2024}.

Consider a hypothesis that is not established by the reference evidence but that the task explicitly permits if it is marked as uncertain.
The evidence judgment can report an unknown evidence state, while the instruction-following judgment can report that the response obeyed the request.
Those two outputs do not by themselves state whether the claim should receive \Hall\ or \LD.
A sufficiently fine-grained instruction-following rubric could encode the same distinction; the contribution of the truth contract is to make the composition rule explicit and inspectable.
The rule covers only this claim-level decision; it is not a general alignment,
creativity, usefulness, or quality measure.

\paragraph{Mapping and worked cases}
First, the purposive mapping examines forty resources from hallucination,
factuality, and creativity evaluation.
For each resource, it records whether the evaluation design represents reference evidence, permission scope, required status marking, and claim-preserving style variation.
Because the sample was selected for conceptual coverage rather than statistical representativeness, the mapping identifies design patterns and missing combinations within the sample; it does not estimate their prevalence in the wider literature.

Second, Section~\ref{sec:cases} presents five worked cases.
Each worked case states what is held fixed, which task or evaluation condition changes, and which change in claim recovery or claim label the rule predicts.
Case~A varies the reference evidence using a documented source-versus-external-evidence distinction in summarization \citep{maynez_faithfulness_2020}.
Case~B changes the discourse frame and the associated contract.
Cases~C--E more narrowly examine required status marking, a permission-scope boundary, and claim-preserving style variation.
Because the worked cases do not form an annotation study, they do not establish
inter-annotator reliability, empirical coverage, or an advantage over simpler
baselines; Section~\ref{sec:agenda} turns these limitations into research
questions.

\paragraph{Contributions}
This position paper makes three contributions.
First, it introduces the truth contract $\TC(p)=(O_p,\Gamma_p,\mu_p)$ and a claim-label rule that separates evidence state, permission scope, and required status marking.
Second, it keeps style-marking level as a separate variable for claim recovery and prompt--response style-marking alignment, then uses a purposive mapping to identify where claim-preserving style variation and claim-level factuality checks remain separated in the selected sample.
Third, it proposes a research agenda for contract-aware benchmarks and mitigation methods that reduce \Hall\ without suppressing \LD\ or supported content at the requested style-marking level.

\paragraph{Organization}
Section~\ref{sec:framework} defines the truth contract, claim recovery,
canonicalization, claim labels, and the prompt--response style-marking
alignment check.
Section~\ref{sec:related} compares the rule with neighboring evaluation paradigms.
Section~\ref{sec:mapping} reports the purposive mapping, and Section~\ref{sec:cases} presents the worked cases.
Sections~\ref{sec:agenda} and~\ref{sec:discussion} present the research agenda, implications, limitations, and falsification criteria.

\section{Contract-Aware Claim Labels and Structured Response-Level Reports}\label{sec:framework}

\subsection{Overview: from a response span to a claim record}

The object of evaluation is a model response $y$ produced under task context $p$.
The \emph{evaluator} may be a human annotator, an automated pipeline, or a
combination of both.
Consider the constructed prompt ``Explain photosynthesis like a poet, but keep the science accurate.''
Suppose the response contains the span ``In each leaf, chlorophyll is a green net for sunlight; captured light drives the reactions that build sugars.''
The evaluator first interprets the span in this task context and recovers two contextualized claims: chlorophyll absorbs light energy, and photosynthesis uses that energy to produce sugars.
It then canonicalizes each claim as
\begin{equation}
c^*=(q^*,\rho^*),
\label{eq:canonical-claim}
\end{equation}
where $q^*$ is its normalized truth-conditional content and $\rho^*$ records
its observed status marking, including epistemic modality and relevant
discourse framing.
The evaluator compares $q^*$ with reference evidence $O_p$ and permission
scope $\Gamma_p$, and compares $\rho^*$ with required status marking $\mu_p$.
If the evidence entails both propositions, each canonical claim receives \SUP.
The evaluator separately compares requested and observed style-marking levels.
This prompt--response style-marking alignment check does not create or excuse a truth-conditional claim.

Claim labeling follows one fixed chain:
\[
\begin{aligned}
\text{response}
&\rightarrow \text{response span}
\rightarrow \text{claim recovery}
\rightarrow \text{contextualized claim}\\
&\rightarrow \text{canonicalization}
\rightarrow \text{canonical claim }c^*=(q^*,\rho^*)
\rightarrow \text{evidence state}\\
&\rightarrow \text{scope and marking tests}
\rightarrow \text{claim label and reason code}.
\end{aligned}
\]
A span-selection, claim recovery, or canonicalization failure is recorded as a
procedural failure and may leave the affected response span without a stable
canonical claim or claim label.
When a stable canonical claim exists, a contract-specification,
evidence-coverage, or adjudication failure leaves its claim label undefined.
The prompt--response style-marking alignment check separately compares $\sigma_p$ with $\widehat{\sigma}(y,p)$.

\subsection{The truth contract}

\paragraph{Truth contract}
For a task context $p$, the truth contract is
\begin{equation}
  \TC(p)=(O_p,\Gamma_p,\mu_p).
\label{eq:truth-contract}
\end{equation}
The three fields answer different questions: against which evidence a
proposition is assessed, which evidence-unknown content the task permits, and
how that content must be marked.
Here, $p$ denotes the relevant task context rather than only the literal prompt $x$.
We drop the subscript $p$ when the task context is clear.
Requested and observed style-marking levels are not truth-contract fields.
The requested level is a task variable used during claim recovery; the
observed level is a response-level measurement used in prompt--response
style-marking alignment.
Only $O_p$, $\Gamma_p$, and $\mu_p$ enter the claim-label rule.

When instructions conflict, the evaluator must state how system instructions
and applicable safety, legal, and domain constraints bound the permission
scope.
A user request cannot override those constraints by redefining reference
evidence or permission scope.

\paragraph{Reference evidence ($O_p$)}
$O_p$ is the reference evidence against which $q^*$ is evaluated, such as a source document, retrieval set, database, gold labels, a specified external reference, or declared fictional-frame truth constraints.
For reproducible evaluation, it also specifies which sources constitute reference evidence and how coverage, temporal validity, and source conflicts are adjudicated.
Existing surveys use different notions of source, context, fact, and world
knowledge \citep{ji_survey_2023,bang_hallulens_2025}.
Reproducible evaluation therefore requires a concrete reference-evidence
specification or an explicit adjudication procedure.

\paragraph{Permission scope ($\Gamma_p$)}
$\Gamma_p$ specifies the topics, entities, claim types, or fictional world in which evidence-unknown content may be introduced.
A factual-only task has an empty permission scope: every truth-conditional claim must be entailed by $O_p$.
For example, a medical brainstorming task may permit possible diagnoses while excluding invented drug doses.
Searle's account of fictional discourse provides indirect support for
distinguishing story-world commitments from assertions presented as
real-world fact \citep{searle_logical_1975}.
We operationalize that distinction through permission scope, which may
include invented characters and events while excluding claims about named
real people.

\paragraph{Required status marking ($\mu_p$)}
$\mu_p$ specifies how the response must signal an evidence-unknown claim.
The signal may be a local hedge, a hypothesis label, a section heading, or a
task-level fictional frame.
Thus, ``this may be early Lyme disease, but testing is required'' satisfies the required status marking, while ``this is early Lyme disease'' does not.
The observed status marking $\rho^*$ is recovered from the complete response and
task context because the relevant marker may appear outside the local span.
The test $M(\rho^*,\mu_p)$ then compares it with the required status marking.

\subsection{Requested and observed style-marking levels}

The requested style-marking level $\sigma_p\in\{0,1,2\}$ records the degree
of style marking requested by the task.
It is a separate task variable available as contextual input during claim
recovery:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $\sigma_p=0$ (neutral/literal): minimal rhetorical transformation and no salient authorial voice;
  \item $\sigma_p=1$ (moderately marked): local metaphor, register shift, or a light stylistic voice without sustained transformation of the response;
  \item $\sigma_p=2$ (strongly marked): sustained figurative language, a salient voice, or substantial stylistic transformation.
\end{itemize}
The levels rank the degree of style marking.
They do not encode the complete qualitative identity of named styles, rank creativity or factuality, or expand permission scope.
Here, \emph{style marking} means a noticeable departure from neutral wording
through deviation or repetition; this operational definition draws on
foregrounding in stylistics
\citep{leech_short_style_1981,vanpeer_stylistics_2020}.
Metaphor is one form of style marking, which may also be lexical, syntactic,
phonological, or discourse-level.
Surface unexpectedness is therefore not, by itself, evidence of factual error.
Style is multi-dimensional, so this three-level scheme is a working discretization rather than a validated universal scale \citep{kang_style_2021,jin_deep_2022}.
The boundaries, especially between $\sigma_p=1$ and $\sigma_p=2$, require empirical validation.

The requested style-marking level $\sigma_p$ must be distinguished from the
observed style-marking level $\widehat{\sigma}(y,p)$.
The prompt--response style-marking alignment check is
\begin{equation}
A_\sigma(y,p)=
\begin{cases}
\textsc{match} & \text{if }\widehat{\sigma}(y,p)=\sigma_p,\\
\textsc{mismatch} & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:style-alignment}
\end{equation}
Together, $\sigma_p$, $\widehat{\sigma}(y,p)$, and $A_\sigma(y,p)$ describe
the requested style-marking level, observed style-marking level, and their
degree-level match.
They remain separate from reference evidence, permission scope, claim labels,
named-style identity, source faithfulness, creativity, factuality, and general
alignment.
For example, a prompt can request $\sigma_p=2$ while the model produces neutral prose with $\widehat{\sigma}(y,p)=0$.
The check then reports \textsc{mismatch}.
If the evaluator cannot determine either level reliably, that difficulty is recorded in $\mathcal F(y,p)$ rather than converted into a claim label.

Requested style-marking level is available as contextual input during claim
recovery, and its effect is tested rather than assumed.
Every recovered claim must remain warranted by linguistic evidence in the produced response, and $\widehat{\sigma}(y,p)$ records the marking actually produced.
Existing evaluators use claim decomposition, natural-language inference, or semantic similarity \citep{min_factscore_2023,chen_menli_2023,aynetdinov_semscore_2024}, while model-based judges used in alignment benchmarking can prioritize style over factuality and safety \citep{feuer_style_2025}.
Whether figurative realization changes which factual claims an evaluator
recovers is therefore a testable hypothesis, not an established result.

\subsection{Unit of analysis, claim recovery, and canonicalization}

We use a claim-level unit because long responses may contain multiple checkable
propositions alongside material that is not independently verifiable
\citep{min_factscore_2023,song_veriscore_2024}.
Let $s$ denote a response span.
Interpreting $s$ within the complete response $y$ and task context $p$ yields a set of contextualized truth-conditional claims $\mathcal C(s,y,p)$.
The same wording can therefore yield different claims in fiction, metaphor, and hypothetical language.

For verification, an annotator canonicalizes each recovered claim as in Equation~\ref{eq:canonical-claim}.
The component $q^*$ records normalized truth-conditional content, including entities, predicates, negation, quantities, temporal scope, and any attribution that changes what is claimed.
The component $\rho^*$ records observed status marking, including relevant
modality and fictional or hypothetical framing.
This separation allows evidence and permission scope to be checked against
$q^*$ without discarding the presentation information needed by $\mu_p$.

Claim recovery follows three rules.
First, commitments that can receive different evidence states are recovered
as separate claims.
Second, attribution, negation, modality, temporal scope, and discourse frame
are preserved in $q^*$ or $\rho^*$ as appropriate.
Third, metaphorical wording does not create a separate claim unless the
response makes an attributable truth-conditional commitment.

If a span yields no truth-conditional claim, it receives no claim label and is
not a procedural failure.
Figurative language is interpreted in the complete response and task context rather than literalized automatically.
Every truth-conditional commitment recovered from the span is canonicalized
before verification.

A pair of matched realizations instantiates \emph{claim-preserving style
variation} when both yield the same canonical claims under fixed reference
evidence $O_p$, permission scope $\Gamma_p$, and required status marking
$\mu_p$.
Only their surface realization and requested or observed style-marking levels
may differ.
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics} gives the formal stability test.

Span-selection, claim recovery, canonicalization, and matching errors are procedural failures.
They may cause an evaluator to drop a recoverable claim, create a spurious canonical claim, or alter $q^*$ or $\rho^*$.
Such errors are recorded in $\mathcal F(y,p)$ and do not themselves receive \SUP, \Hall, or \LD.
If no stable canonical claim can be obtained, no claim label is assigned to the
affected span.
A failure to realize the requested style-marking level is recorded through
$A_\sigma(y,p)$; a canonical claim that violates the truth contract is handled
by the claim-label rule.

\subsection{Evidence state, claim label, and structured response-level report}

A claim label is assigned only after the truth contract has been specified, a
stable canonical claim obtained, and reference-evidence coverage and
adjudication judged adequate.
A claim recovery or canonicalization failure may prevent a stable $c^*$ from
being formed; the evaluator records that failure in $\mathcal F(y,p)$ and
assigns no claim label to the affected response span.
If a stable $c^*$ exists but contract specification, reference-evidence
coverage, or adjudication fails, the evaluator records that failure in
$\mathcal F(y,p)$ and leaves $V(c^*\mid y,p)$ undefined.

When these prerequisites hold, record the evidence state of each canonical
claim $c^*=(q^*,\rho^*)$:
\begin{equation}
E(q^*,O_p)\in
\{\textsc{entailed},\textsc{contradicted},\textsc{unknown}\}.
\label{eq:evidence-state}
\end{equation}
The last value means that $O_p$ neither entails nor contradicts $q^*$; it is not a statement about all possible knowledge.
Evidence state alone does not determine the claim label.

Let $L(q^*,\Gamma_p)\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether the evidence-unknown
proposition lies inside the permission scope.
Let $M(\rho^*,\mu_p)\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether its observed status marking
satisfies the required status marking.
Only a claim with evidence state \textsc{unknown} proceeds to these permission and marking tests.
Then
\begin{equation}
V(c^*\mid y,p)=
\begin{cases}
\SUP
  & \text{if }E(q^*,O_p)=\textsc{entailed},\\[1mm]
\LD
  & \text{if }E(q^*,O_p)=\textsc{unknown},\ L=1,\ M=1,\\[1mm]
\Hall
  & \text{if }E(q^*,O_p)=\textsc{contradicted},\\[1mm]
\Hall
  & \text{if }E(q^*,O_p)=\textsc{unknown},\ L=0,\\[1mm]
\Hall
  & \text{if }E(q^*,O_p)=\textsc{unknown},\ L=1,\ M=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:claim-label}
\end{equation}

When $V(c^*\mid y,p)=\Hall$, attach one mutually exclusive reason code:
\begin{equation}
r_{\Hall}(c^*\mid y,p)=
\begin{cases}
\textsc{contradicted}
  & \text{if }E(q^*,O_p)=\textsc{contradicted},\\[1mm]
\textsc{out-of-scope}
  & \text{if }E(q^*,O_p)=\textsc{unknown},\ L=0,\\[1mm]
\textsc{marking-failure}
  & \text{if }E(q^*,O_p)=\textsc{unknown},\ L=1,\ M=0.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:hallucination-reason}
\end{equation}
No hallucination reason code is attached to \SUP\ or \LD.
The style-marking variables do not appear in either rule because they provide context during claim recovery and are assessed separately through $A_\sigma(y,p)$.

\SUP\ means \emph{supported}: $O_p$ entails $q^*$.
\Hall\ means \emph{hallucination}: $q^*$ is contradicted by $O_p$, or it is evidence-unknown and fails the permission-scope or required-status-marking test.
\LD\ means \emph{licensed divergence}: $O_p$ leaves $q^*$ unknown, while $q^*$
lies inside $\Gamma_p$ and $\rho^*$ satisfies $\mu_p$.
An \LD\ label records that the permission-scope and required-status-marking
conditions are met, not that the proposition is true relative to $O_p$.
A \textsc{marking-failure} receives \Hall\ because the evidence-unknown claim
is not authorized in the form in which the response presents it.

A claim that contradicts $O_p$ always receives \Hall\ once the procedural prerequisites are satisfied.
For fiction, $O_p$ includes the declared fictional-frame truth constraints; disagreement with the real world alone does not make an in-frame invention contradicted.
If an entailed claim violates an independent hedge or style instruction, it remains \SUP\ and the separate instruction-following evaluation records the violation.

An explicit refusal or a statement such as ``the available evidence is insufficient'' is evaluated as a meta-claim about the evidence.
It is not automatically treated as asserting the embedded proposition.
Treating the refusal or insufficiency statement as a meta-claim prevents
calibrated abstention from being mislabeled as hallucination.

Let the defined claim trace be
\begin{equation}
\mathcal T(y,p)
=
\{(c_i^*,V_i,r_i)\mid V_i\ \text{is defined}\},
\label{eq:claim-trace}
\end{equation}
where $r_i=r_{\Hall}(c_i^*\mid y,p)$ when $V_i=\Hall$ and $r_i=\varnothing$ otherwise.
Claims affected by procedural failures are omitted from $\mathcal T(y,p)$ and
recorded in $\mathcal F(y,p)$.
For every response, the structured response-level report is
\begin{equation}
R(y\mid p)=
\left\langle
\mathcal T(y,p),
\mathcal F(y,p),
\operatorname{Cov}(y,p),
A_\sigma(y,p),
S(y,p),
U(y,p)
\right\rangle ,
\label{eq:response-record}
\end{equation}
where $\operatorname{Cov}(y,p)$ is the claim-coverage diagnostic, $S(y,p)$ is severity when assessed, and $U(y,p)$ is task-relative usefulness when assessed.
$U(y,p)$ records usefulness: the assessed value of authorized content for the
task objective, at a stated unit such as the claim or complete response.
The structured response-level report publishes the claim trace, the
distribution of \SUP, \Hall, and \LD, procedural failures, and claim coverage.
When assessed, prompt--response style-marking alignment, severity, and
usefulness remain separately named diagnostics rather than components of a
mandatory scalar score.
$R(y\mid p)$ is a structured response-level report, not a scalar aggregation;
any later scalar or ranking rule must state explicitly which components it
uses.

\paragraph{Structured response-level report versus scalar aggregation}
Equation~\ref{eq:response-record} keeps claim labels, procedural failures,
coverage, prompt--response style-marking alignment, severity, and usefulness
visible in $R(y\mid p)$.
This report does not impose a scalar response score.
Existing factuality evaluation reports both claim-aggregated scores and
benchmark-level decisions \citep{min_factscore_2023,bayat_factbench_2024}.
Any such aggregation answers an additional task-dependent question and should
be reported together with the underlying structured response-level report.

\section{Related Evaluation Paradigms}\label{sec:related}

The rule combines four components that neighboring paradigms treat separately.
Factuality and source faithfulness define evidence relations; intent-aware
evaluation represents broader task constraints; creativity research supplies
separate usefulness criteria; and claim-level evaluation supplies the unit of
analysis.
The truth contract addresses the decision that follows an \textsc{unknown}
evidence state: whether permission scope and required status marking yield
\LD\ or \Hall.

\subsection{Hallucination taxonomies and claim labels}

Hallucination surveys organize the phenomenon along several related but non-equivalent axes.
\citet{ji_survey_2023} distinguish intrinsic hallucinations, which contradict the source, from extrinsic hallucinations, which cannot be verified from it.
\citet{huang_survey_2025} separate factuality hallucinations---factual contradiction or fabrication---from faithfulness hallucinations involving instruction, context, or logical inconsistency.
\citet{zhang_sirens_2025} instead distinguish input-, context-, and fact-conflicting outputs.
Other reviews organize causes across the model lifecycle or examine factuality and faithfulness metrics for open-ended generation \citep{wang_factuality_survey_2024,malin_faithfulness_review_2025,lamba_lifecycle_2026}.
From a creativity perspective, \citet{jiang_survey_2024} distinguish divergent generation from convergent evaluation.

These surveys classify mismatches by their evidence relation, inconsistency type, source, cause, or evaluation setting.
The truth contract addresses a different decision: whether an evidence-unknown
claim falls inside $\Gamma_p$ and satisfies $\mu_p$.
The resulting claim label should therefore remain distinct from the
descriptive taxonomy.

\subsection{Evidence relations}
Source faithfulness asks whether a claim is supported by provided evidence, whereas factuality checks it against an external reference \citep{maynez_faithfulness_2020,kryscinski_evaluating_2020}.
Both instantiate the evidence relation under different choices of $O_p$.
Permission scope and required status marking instead determine whether an
evidence-unknown claim in a response is authorized and marked as required.

\subsection{Assertion, commitment, and contextualization}
Assertion is treated here as a public commitment to propositional content
\citep{stalnaker_assertion_1978}.
Because interpretation depends on conversational context, claim recovery
retains the context needed to attribute that commitment
\citep{clark_using_1996}.
Canonicalization may add context needed to make a claim stand alone, but it
must preserve the original meaning \citep{choi_decontextualization_2021}.
Hedges can qualify categorical commitment \citep{hyland_hedging_1996}; their
observed contribution is recorded in $\rho^*$.
These sources motivate contextualized claims and canonicalization; they do not
validate the proposed categories of required status marking.

\subsection{Claim extraction methods and claim recovery}
External claim-extraction methods decompose responses into checkable units
before verification
\citep{min_factscore_2023,song_veriscore_2024,metropolitansky_claimify_2025}.
Coverage errors omit attributable commitments, whereas atomicity errors merge
propositions that could receive different evidence states
\citep{metropolitansky_claimify_2025,wanner_closer_2024}.
Decontextualization can remove attribution, modality, or discourse framing,
and canonicalization can alter $q^*$ or $\rho^*$
\citep{wanner_closer_2024,choi_decontextualization_2021}.
These methods supply claim-extraction machinery, but claim recovery and
canonicalization are evaluated separately before claim labels are assigned.

\subsection{Intent, instruction-following evaluation, and usefulness}
Intent-aware evaluation checks whether a response satisfies query constraints
\citep{hao_faithqa_2025}.
The contract rule isolates the reference-evidence, permission-scope, and
required-status-marking decisions at claim level; it does not replace broader
instruction-following evaluation.
Safety, usefulness, and overall response quality remain separate assessments.

Recent work distinguishes potentially valuable or ``intelligent'' hallucinations from defective ones \citep{jiang_survey_2024,sui_confabulation_2024,yang_hicbench_2025}.
Our distinction does not rest on value.
Permission scope and usefulness answer different questions.
Permission scope is evaluated first: an ingenious fabricated medical fact remains \Hall, whereas an unhelpful but in-frame fictional invention remains \LD\ with low usefulness.

\subsection{Claim-preserving style variation}
Stylistics and style-transfer research examine changes in linguistic form
intended to preserve content
\citep{leech_short_style_1981,vanpeer_stylistics_2020,pavlick_empirical_2016,rao_dear_2018,briakou_evaluating_2021}.
Hallucination surveys and benchmarks, by contrast, primarily organize evidence
relations such as support, contradiction, or unverifiability
\citep{ji_survey_2023,huang_survey_2025,bang_hallulens_2025}.
We therefore propose a controlled test for hallucination evaluation: vary
requested style-marking level while holding canonical claims and reference
evidence fixed, then test whether claim recovery and claim labels remain
stable.
The purposive mapping in Section~\ref{sec:mapping} records whether the selected
resources support this test.

\section{Purposive Mapping of Evaluation Resources}\label{sec:mapping}

We purposively selected forty evaluation resources for conceptual coverage
across factual question answering, grounded generation, intent evaluation,
creative writing, divergent thinking, and constrained problem solving.
The resulting purposive mapping is an inspectable description of these
resources, not a systematic review, representative sample, or prevalence
estimate.
Because this position paper does not document a search date, search queries,
or an independent recoding procedure, every descriptive claim is restricted
to the selected sample and to our coding of it.

\subsection{Mapping protocol}

For each resource, we recorded seven questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What reference evidence $O$, if any, is specified for assessing claim support?
  \item What quality or task-success criterion, if any, is used separately from reference evidence?
  \item Does the protocol vary or score requested style-marking level $\sigma$ while controlling claim content?
  \item Which nominal task profile best describes it: strict grounding, intent
        evaluation, declared-frame invention and ideation, constrained solution
        search, or heterogeneous suites and tool use?
  \item How does the protocol represent permission scope $\Gamma$?
  \item Does it score required status marking $\mu$ separately?
  \item Does it test whether claim-preserving style variation changes claim
        recovery or claim labels?
\end{enumerate}

Appendix~\ref{app:mapping} reports the resource-level coding using a closed
vocabulary defined before the tables.
Reference evidence is kept separate from quality, preference, feasibility,
correctness, and test-based success criteria: the latter are not treated as
$O$ unless the protocol uses them to establish or contradict a factual claim.
The profiles are nominal descriptions and never enter the claim-label rule.

\subsection{Observed pattern in the sample}

The complete coding of the selected sample appears in Appendix
Tables~\ref{tab:full-mapping-factual-a}--\ref{tab:full-mapping-creative}.

\paragraph{Strict grounding resources}
Among the twenty-one resources assigned to the strict grounding profile in our
coding, reference evidence is generally specified, requested style-marking
level is not separately controlled, permission scope is coded as inferred
empty, and required status marking is not separately scored.
The selected resource assigned to intent evaluation scores satisfaction of
query constraints, but does not separately represent permission scope or
required status marking.

\paragraph{Declared-frame invention and ideation; constrained solution search}
Within the selected creative-writing resources, general writing quality,
creativity, or linguistic complexity is sometimes evaluated, but this is not
equivalent to controlling requested style-marking level.
No selected creative-writing resource holds canonical claim content fixed
while manipulating that level.
The selected divergent-thinking resources likewise leave requested
style-marking level uncontrolled.
The five selected constrained solution search resources use feasibility,
correctness, specifications, tests, or task-specific creativity criteria as
task-success criteria; these criteria are reported separately from reference
evidence.

\paragraph{Coverage pattern in the selected sample}
In our coding of this selected sample, no resource independently varies
reference evidence, requested style-marking level, permission scope, and
required status marking.
No selected resource jointly evaluates claim recovery under
claim-preserving style variation, prompt--response style-marking alignment, and
downstream claim labels.
The selected factuality and faithfulness resources also do not test whether
claim-preserving style variation changes claim recovery or claim labels.
Thus, the selected sample contains no strict grounding resource with
controlled claim-preserving style variation.
A controlled benchmark would allow this possible failure mode to be measured
directly; whether the selected verifiers exhibit it remains an open
question.

\section{Worked Cases}\label{sec:cases}

The five comparisons below state what is held fixed, what changes, and which
result the rule predicts.
Case~A instantiates a documented distinction in summarization; Cases~B--E are
constructed specification checks.
They clarify boundary conditions but do not establish annotation reliability,
empirical coverage, or superiority to simpler baselines.

\paragraph{Case A: changing the reference evidence}
In XSum, a summary statement may be absent from the source document while
remaining correct against external evidence \citep{maynez_faithfulness_2020}.
\emph{Fixed:} the response span, canonical claim, and empty permission scope.
\emph{Changed:} $O_p$ is either the source document or an external evidence set.
\emph{Outcome:} the claim receives \Hall\ with reason
\textsc{out-of-scope} when it is evidence-unknown relative to the source under
$\Gamma_p=\varnothing$, and \SUP\ when the external evidence entails it.

\paragraph{Case B: changing the discourse frame}
\emph{Fixed:} the constructed string ``Caf\'e Verdier existed in 1920s Paris.''
\emph{Changed:} the historical task treats a register with no Verdier entry as
exhaustive, whereas the fiction task uses declared story-world constraints;
the discourse frame, $O_p$, and $\Gamma_p$ therefore change.
\emph{Outcome:} the historical task yields a real-world canonical
claim labeled \Hall\ with reason \textsc{contradicted}, whereas the fiction
task yields an evidence-unknown story-world canonical claim labeled \LD.
The comparison demonstrates that identical strings need not express identical
contextualized claims.

\paragraph{Case C: changing the required status marking}
Suppose a task permits version-specific hypotheses about a tool but requires
uncertainty to be marked when the documentation is inconclusive.
\emph{Fixed:} $O_p$, $\Gamma_p$, $\mu_p$, the proposition that the tool
supports several formats, and its evidence-unknown state.
\emph{Changed:} only the observed status marking $\rho^*$.
\emph{Outcome:} ``the tool supports several formats'' receives \Hall\
with reason \textsc{marking-failure}, whereas ``the tool may support several
formats, but this requires version-specific verification'' receives \LD.

\paragraph{Case D: testing a scope boundary}
\emph{Fixed:} a task context that permits invention inside a declared fictional
frame and satisfies the frame-level required status marking.
\emph{Changed:} the claim concerns either a fictional entity or a named real
person presented as real history.
\emph{Outcome:} the in-frame fictional claim can receive \LD, whereas
the real-world claim falls outside $\Gamma_p$ and receives \Hall\ with reason
\textsc{out-of-scope}.

\paragraph{Case E: changing surface realization with a fixed claim}
\emph{Fixed:} $O_p$, $\Gamma_p$, $\mu_p$, and the canonical claim that
photosynthesis uses light energy to produce sugars.
\emph{Changed:} only requested style-marking level $\sigma_p$ and surface
realization; the two responses are designed as a claim-preserving style
variation.
\emph{Outcome:} both variants recover the same canonical claim and
receive \SUP\ when $O_p$ entails it; $\widehat{\sigma}(y,p)$ and
prompt--response style-marking alignment are reported separately.
A difference in recovered claims or labels would mean that the evaluator
failed this claim-preserving style-variation test.
It would not, by itself, establish that style generally changes claim recovery.

Together, the cases isolate changes in reference evidence, discourse frame,
required status marking, permission scope, and surface realization.
Their predictions define tests for the annotation and benchmark studies below.

\section{Research Agenda}\label{sec:agenda}

The agenda follows a validation sequence.
Priorities~1--3 ask whether the truth contract can be annotated, manipulated,
and used to diagnose pipeline errors.
Priorities~4 and~5 examine response-level evaluation and mitigation, and
Priority~6 tests external validity.
Priority~7 examines temporal updates and agentic action.
Across Priorities~1--7, contract-aware evaluation should be compared with a
baseline that combines factuality and instruction-following evaluation.
To avoid circular evaluation, studies should use independently adjudicated
gold claim labels, apply the same claim recovery procedure and reference
evidence to the contract-aware method and its baseline, and specify decision
thresholds before evaluation.

\paragraph{1. Contract annotation, inference, and reliability}
\emph{Research problem.}
The first unresolved question is whether evaluators can identify $O_p$,
$\Gamma_p$, and $\mu_p$ consistently from the task context and annotate
$\sigma_p$ separately.
\emph{Why it threatens the position.}
This matters because disagreement over a claim label may reflect
incompatible readings of the task rather than a substantive difference in
reference evidence or permission scope.
\emph{Study and measures.}
A public codebook should define contextualized claims, evidence states,
permission scope, required status marking, requested and observed
style-marking levels, and claim-preserving style variation.
Pilot studies should annotate each field separately, preserve genuine
disagreement, and report agreement by field and label, adjudication rate,
annotation time, and disagreement sources.
Contract-inference studies should also document how user requests, system
instructions, domain constraints, and safety or legal rules are reconciled,
and how clarifications or corrections update the contract in multi-turn
interaction.
Because $O_p$, $\Gamma_p$, and $\mu_p$ are specified at task-context level,
their annotation cost can be amortized across responses evaluated under the
same task context.
Claim recovery, canonicalization, and evidence adjudication remain
per-response costs.
\emph{Result that would support or weaken the position.}
Reliable field-level annotations at a feasible cost would support the
position; persistent disagreement or disproportionate cost would weaken it.

\paragraph{2. Factorial benchmark design}
\emph{Research problem.}
Naturally occurring evaluation data often change reference evidence, permission
scope, required status marking, and surface realization together, which
prevents attribution of a claim-label change to one field.
\emph{Why it threatens the position.}
Without controlled contrasts, the proposed composition rule cannot be
distinguished from a baseline combining factuality and instruction-following
evaluation.
\emph{Study and measures.}
A controlled factorial benchmark should therefore vary one of $O_p$,
$\Gamma_p$, or $\mu_p$ while holding the remaining fields and target claim
fixed.
Separate claim-preserving style variation pairs should realize matched
canonical claims at
$\sigma_p\in\{0,1,2\}$ while keeping $O_p$, $\Gamma_p$, and $\mu_p$ fixed.
These contrasts test a hypothesis about claim recovery; they do not assume
that style changes the recovered claim set.
Evaluation should report claim-set equivalence, evidence state, claim label,
accuracy of observed style-marking level annotation, prompt--response
style-marking alignment, and performance of the baseline combining factuality
and instruction-following evaluation.
Gold claim labels should be adjudicated independently, both methods should use
the same claim recovery procedure and reference evidence, and calibration and
decision thresholds should be fixed before evaluation.
\emph{Result that would support or weaken the position.}
Predictable claim-label changes when
$O_p$, $\Gamma_p$, or $\mu_p$ changes, together with stable labels for
claim-preserving style variation, would support the position.
Failure to preserve factual commitment across variants, or equal diagnostic
stability from the baseline, would weaken it.

\paragraph{3. Style-robust claim recovery and stage-specific diagnosis}
\emph{Research problem.}
An incorrect claim label may originate in span selection, claim recovery,
canonicalization, observed style-marking level annotation, evidence assessment,
or application of $\Gamma_p$ and $\mu_p$.
\emph{Why it threatens the position.}
An end-to-end hallucination score cannot identify which stage produced the
error, so an apparent advantage may arise from a different upstream
claim recovery decision.
\emph{Study and measures.}
Claim recovery and verification pipelines should therefore be tested on
matched variants at requested style-marking levels $0$, $1$, and $2$
containing metaphor, irony, analogy, narrative voice, hypotheses, or declared
fiction.
For each response, the evaluation trace should record span type, canonical
claims, evidence states, scope and marking decisions, claim labels, observed
style-marking level, and prompt--response style-marking alignment.
Comparisons among claim extraction, natural-language inference, retrieval, and
model-judge pipelines should report stage-specific accuracy and calibration at
each requested style-marking level.
The same claim recovery procedure should also be tested with and without
access to the requested style-marking level while the response, reference
evidence, permission scope, and required status marking remain fixed.
\emph{Result that would support or weaken the position.}
Stable evidence states and claim labels for matched canonical claims, together
with reproducible stage-specific error attribution, would support the
position; no consequential diagnostic gain over end-to-end evaluation would
weaken it.

\paragraph{4. Use of the structured response-level report}
\emph{Research problem.}
Claim labels do not determine how complete responses should be compared.
\emph{Why it threatens the position.}
A single rate can hide claim coverage, the distribution of \SUP, \Hall, and
\LD, and the difference between one severe violation and several
low-consequence errors.
\emph{Study and measures.}
Studies should first publish $R(y\mid p)$ as defined in
Equation~\ref{eq:response-record}, then compare optional secondary aggregation
rules such as the proportion of \Hall\ claims, worst-case aggregation,
severity-weighted scoring, and usefulness-aware scoring.
Every aggregate should be accompanied by the underlying claim trace, claim
coverage, prompt--response style-marking alignment, and sensitivity to the
aggregation rule.
The comparison should include tasks with different consequences, because a
medical claim labeled \Hall\ and a harmless fictional detail should not
receive the same response-level weight.
\emph{Result that would support or weaken the position.}
An aggregation would support the position if it enables meaningful response
comparisons without obscuring the claims and contract conditions that produced
the score; sensitivity that reverses conclusions without an explicit
task-dependent rationale would weaken it.

\paragraph{5. Mitigation without suppressing licensed divergence or supported content at the requested style-marking level}
\emph{Research problem.}
A mitigation method can lower a conventional hallucination rate by increasing
abstention, avoiding figurative language, or suppressing evidence-unknown
content indiscriminately.
\emph{Why it threatens the position.}
Such a reduction would not show whether reliability improved or the model
merely became more conservative.
\emph{Study and measures.}
Retrieval, verification, abstention, and constrained decoding should therefore
be evaluated on three outcomes: reduction of \Hall\ under grounded contracts,
preservation of useful \LD\ under permissive contracts, and preservation of
supported content at $\sigma_p=1$ or $2$.
Because usefulness is one criterion in creativity judgments and creative-text
judgments vary across readers, usefulness should remain a separate score and
report uncertainty \citep{diedrich_are_2015,marco_reader_2025}.
Per-contract reporting or Pareto comparisons should expose trade-offs among
the three outcomes.
\emph{Result that would support or weaken the position.}
A mitigation would support the position if it reduces claims labeled \Hall\
without systematically removing claims labeled \LD\ or supported content at
the requested style-marking level; indiscriminate suppression would weaken it.

\paragraph{6. Generalization across domains and languages}
\emph{Research problem.}
The contract fields and three-level style-marking scale may work in the
constructed examples yet fail when evidence standards, hedging conventions,
genres, or instruction hierarchies change.
\emph{Why it threatens the position.}
Systematic domain- or language-specific disagreement would limit the claimed
generality of the framework.
\emph{Study and measures.}
The annotation and contrast protocols should therefore be tested in
high-stakes factual domains, education, journalism, scientific communication,
brainstorming, and fiction.
Multilingual studies should examine whether style marking, epistemic
hedges, and genre conventions support comparable annotations across languages
and cultures.
\emph{Result that would support or weaken the position.}
Interpretable contract fields and stable diagnoses beyond the single-turn
English examples would support the position; systematic disagreement would
weaken it and identify where the formulation requires revision.

\paragraph{7. Temporal truth-contract updates and agentic action}
\emph{Research problem.}
In multi-step interaction, reference evidence and applicable instructions may
change over time, while later actions may consume earlier claims.
\emph{Why it threatens the position.}
A static truth contract cannot represent an update to $O_t$ or show how a
\textsc{marking-failure} propagates from a claim into an action.
\emph{Study and measures.}
Agentic studies should model time-indexed reference evidence $O_t$, assign a
task-level subcontract to each subtask, and trace claim labels and reason codes
into downstream actions.
Here, a task-level subcontract is the truth contract associated with one
subtask.
They should measure update accuracy, cross-subtask error propagation, and
whether an action relies on a claim that failed required status marking.
\emph{Result that would support or weaken the position.}
Reproducible traces that localize when a truth-contract update or claim-level
failure changes an action would support the position; failure to obtain such
traces would weaken it.


\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Evaluation implications}

A response can contain no \Hall\ yet have low usefulness or fail
prompt--response style-marking alignment.
Conversely, usefulness and prompt--response style-marking alignment do not
change a claim label.

Severity also remains distinct from the claim label.
A false drug dose and an incorrect trivia date can both receive \Hall, although
their consequences differ.
Severity may therefore weight a violation after labeling, but it does not
determine whether the violation occurred.

\paragraph{Avoiding indiscriminate suppression}
\citet{kalai_calibrated_2024} derive a statistical lower bound for
hallucination on certain arbitrary facts under a generative-model calibration
condition.
\citet{xu_hallucination_2024} prove an impossibility result for computable
models used as general problem solvers over computable ground-truth functions.
\citet{banerjee_llms_2024} argue for structural non-zero hallucination risk
from computability and undecidability limits.
These results rely on different assumptions; none alone implies a constant
deployment error rate or precludes statistically negligible rates in a
specified setting
\citep{suzuki_negligible_2025}.
Suppressing every departure from the reference evidence would also suppress
hypotheses and in-frame invention.
Empirical and conceptual work reports possible trade-offs between hallucination
mitigation and creativity
\citep{banerjee_does_2025,sun_hallucinating_2024,he_shakespearean_2025}.
Evaluation should therefore report reductions in \Hall, preservation of \LD,
and retention of supported content at the requested style-marking level
separately.

\subsection{Limitations and safeguards}

The proposal has six immediate limitations.
First, an ambiguous or inconsistent task context may prevent stable
truth-contract specification.
The evaluator must state the precedence of system and user instructions and
applicable safety, legal, and domain constraints; otherwise it records a
contract-specification failure and leaves the affected claim label undefined.

Second, incomplete reference-evidence coverage or unresolved source conflict
may make the evidence state unreliable.
The safeguard is a coverage or adjudication failure, not an automatic
\textsc{unknown} evidence state.

Third, span selection, claim recovery, and canonicalization require judgment.
Validation therefore requires independently adjudicated canonical claims and
separate reporting of procedural failures.

Fourth, permission scope must be specified from the task context before claim
labeling and cannot be widened after inspecting a response to turn a
fabrication into \LD.
User-specified permission scope also remains bounded by applicable safety, legal, and
domain constraints.

Fifth, the three-level style-marking scale is not a validated universal model
of style, and an effect of requested style-marking level on claim recovery
remains a testable hypothesis.

Sixth, the purposive mapping describes the authors' coding of the selected
resources.
It has no documented independent recoding and cannot establish prevalence
across the broader literature.


\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

This position paper argues that non-entailment alone should not determine a
hallucination claim label.
The truth contract $\TC(p)=(O_p,\Gamma_p,\mu_p)$ separates reference evidence,
permission scope, and required status marking.
A canonical claim whose proposition $q^*$ is evidence-unknown receives \LD\
only when $q^*$ falls inside $\Gamma_p$ and its observed status marking
$\rho^*$ satisfies $\mu_p$; otherwise it receives a reason-coded \Hall.
Requested and observed style-marking levels remain separate variables for claim
recovery and prompt--response style-marking alignment, and never authorize
content.
Procedural failures, usefulness, and severity remain separate diagnostics
within the structured response-level report.

Within the purposive mapping, no selected factuality or creativity resource
jointly controls the three truth-contract fields and requested style-marking
level.
The immediate test is whether contract-aware annotation improves agreement or
diagnostic stability over a baseline combining factuality and
instruction-following evaluation on matched claims.

\appendixsection{Additional Diagnostics and Optional Aggregation}%
\phantomsection
\makeatletter\def\@currentlabel{A}\makeatother\label{app:diagnostics}

Section~\ref{sec:framework} defines the truth contract, claim recovery,
canonicalization, claim-label rule, and prompt--response style-marking
alignment check.
This appendix records the additional diagnostics needed for an empirical implementation.

\paragraph{Claim-preserving style variation test}
Consider three expert-adjudicated responses $y_0,y_1,y_2$ produced under task
contexts that differ only in requested style-marking level, with
$\sigma_{p_0}=0$, $\sigma_{p_1}=1$, and $\sigma_{p_2}=2$.
After one-to-one adjudicated canonical matching, let
$\mathcal C^*(y_i,p_i)$ denote each set of canonical claims
$c^*=(q^*,\rho^*)$.
The responses instantiate claim-preserving style variation only if the matched
sets contain the same $q^*$ and $\rho^*$ components; their surface strings and
observed style-marking levels may differ.
With fixed $O_p$, $\Gamma_p$, and $\mu_p$, the desired stability condition is
\[
\mathcal C^*(y_0,p_0)=\mathcal C^*(y_1,p_1)=\mathcal C^*(y_2,p_2),
\qquad
V(c_j^*\mid y_0,p_0)=V(c_j^*\mid y_1,p_1)=V(c_j^*\mid y_2,p_2)
\]
for every matched canonical claim $c_j^*$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Claim recovery error}: a recoverable claim is dropped or a
        spurious claim is added during claim recovery.
  \item \emph{Canonicalization error}: canonicalization alters $q^*$ or
        $\rho^*$.
  \item \emph{Claim-label instability}: matched canonical claims receive
        different labels when $O_p$, $\Gamma_p$, and $\mu_p$ are fixed.
  \item \emph{Prompt--response style-marking alignment error}: the evaluator
        incorrectly classifies the match between requested and observed
        style-marking levels.
  \item \emph{Secondary aggregation sensitivity}: a response comparison
        changes when the evaluator changes an optional rule applied to
        $R(y\mid p)$.
\end{itemize}

Claim recovery and claim-label diagnostics require expert-adjudicated canonical
claim matches.
Prompt--response style-marking alignment errors require an independently
adjudicated comparison between requested and observed style-marking levels.
Claim-label instability is an error only when $O_p$, $\Gamma_p$, and $\mu_p$ remain fixed.
When $\Gamma_p$ or $\mu_p$ changes, the same evidence-unknown claim may
intentionally change from \Hall\ to \LD; the evidence state remains
\textsc{unknown}.

For a complete response $y$, an evaluation should publish the structured response-level report
$R(y\mid p)$ before applying any scalar or ranking rule.
Comparisons should report sensitivity to plausible secondary aggregation rules
rather than present one rule as uniquely determined.

  \emph{specified environment or tool state};
  \emph{task-dependent or mixed};
  \emph{none identified}.
  This field records evidence usable for claim support, not a general quality
  or task-success criterion.

  \item[Quality or task-success criterion.]
  \emph{not separately represented};
  \emph{quality or preference rubric};
  \emph{novelty or divergent-thinking rubric};
  \emph{feasibility or correctness criterion};
  \emph{specification or tests};
  \emph{mixed}.

  \item[Requested style-marking treatment.]
  \emph{not controlled};
  \emph{not controlled or not applicable};
  \emph{scored, not claim-controlled}.

  \item[Permission scope $\Gamma$.]
  \emph{inferred empty};
  \emph{implicit frame};
  \emph{partial};
  \emph{task-specific};
  \emph{subtask-specific};
  \emph{mixed};
  \emph{not separately represented}.
  A specification, test, or query constraint is not coded as permission scope
  unless the protocol uses it to authorize evidence-unknown content.

  \item[Coding status.]
  Each resource name carries one superscript from a closed vocabulary:
  \textsuperscript{X} (\emph{explicit in the source}),
  \textsuperscript{I} (\emph{inferred from task design}),
  \textsuperscript{P} (\emph{partial}), or
  \textsuperscript{H} (\emph{heterogeneous}).
  The superscript is a row-level summary of the resource's evaluation setup,
  not a provenance marker for every table cell.
  A cell explicitly described as \emph{inferred} remains an inference even
  when the resource-level superscript is \textsuperscript{X}.

  \item[Required status marking $\mu$.]
  \emph{not scored} means that required status marking is not evaluated as a
  separate dimension.
  \emph{not separated} means that a relevant required-status-marking criterion
  is absorbed into a broader task or rubric rather than coded independently.
\end{description}

These codes summarize our interpretation of the selected protocols.
They do not imply that a source uses the truth-contract terminology itself.

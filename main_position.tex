\begin{abstract}
A model writing a museum label adds one sentence: a plausible reconstruction
that the source neither confirms nor denies. Under a strict-grounding brief,
that sentence is a hallucination. Under a brief inviting one clearly marked
conjecture, it is what was asked for. The sentence, the source, and the
evidence are identical; only the task differs.

We argue that hallucination is relative to a task's \emph{truth contract}. The
contract changes the label, not the proposition's truth. Evidence-only
protocols leave that distinction implicit and cannot act on it by themselves.
A truth contract makes three task conditions explicit: the \emph{task oracle}
that settles claims, the \emph{permission scope} for evidence-unknown content,
and the \emph{required status marking} under which it may appear.

Labeling then takes two steps rather than one. The task oracle first assigns an
evidence state. Entailed claims are grounded and contradicted claims are
hallucinations. Only unknown claims proceed to the permission-scope and
required-status-marking checks, which separate hallucination from \emph{licensed
divergence}, a label that records compliance rather than truth. Claim labels
compose into a response-level record rather than into a single score.

The rule is deliberately narrow: it labels individual claims, and verifies
neither reasoning, coherence, completeness, nor relevance. In a purposive
mapping of forty-one resources, no selected design makes all three fields
separately visible or holds claim wording fixed while they vary; this diagnoses
the selected designs, not the gap's prevalence. Five worked cases show which
field must change to move a label. The position would be weakened if a combined
factuality and instruction-following baseline matched contract-aware annotation
on claim-label agreement, reason-code accuracy, and error localization.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Large language models (LLMs) can produce fluent claims that a task's available
information contradicts or leaves unsettled
\citep{ji_survey_2023,huang_survey_2025}.
Such outputs can be errors in strict-grounding settings and can limit the use
of LLMs in high-stakes applications.
Accordingly, research spans taxonomies, benchmarks, detection, and mitigation
\citep{ji_survey_2023,huang_survey_2025}.

A claim left unknown by the available information is not always an error.
Fiction permits invention, and brainstorming can solicit novel suggestions
rather than reproduction of available evidence
\citep{franceschelli_creativity_2024,jiang_survey_2024,rick_supermind_2023}.
A hypothesis-generation task may likewise authorize explicitly marked
propositions that the evidence leaves unknown.
A clinical assistant asked what a patient's symptoms might indicate should be
able to raise early Lyme disease as a possibility, but must not present it as
established.

These tasks separate two questions: does the available information settle the
claim, and did the task authorize it? We argue that hallucination judgments
need both, whereas many evidence-based protocols operationalize only the first.
Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-to-claim} illustrates the contrast with the museum-label
task used throughout the paper.

Factuality evaluation compares generated claims with external knowledge
sources, and source-faithfulness evaluation compares them with a provided
source document
\citep{maynez_faithfulness_2020,kryscinski_evaluating_2020,min_factscore_2023}.
In the framework developed below, these resources instantiate the task oracle.
Creativity research commonly distinguishes novelty from usefulness or quality
\citep{runco_standard_2012,diedrich_are_2015};
CreativityPrism, for example, evaluates quality, novelty, and diversity
\citep{hou_creativityprism_2025}, and recent surveys and task-specific novelty
metrics continue to refine these criteria
\citep{ismayilzada_progress_2024,zhao_assessing_2025,namuduri_genie_2026}.
These dimensions do not by themselves determine whether a novel factual claim
violates a task constraint.
Consequently, two protocols can assign different labels to identical surface wording when their prompts establish different task conditions.

\paragraph{Our position}
We propose a claim-level evaluation rule under which the hallucination label is
relative to the task's truth contract. By \emph{relative}, we mean that the
evaluation label depends on the contract; the truth value of the underlying
proposition does not.

A \emph{claim} is a truth-conditional commitment attributed to the response:
something the response presents as being the case and that can therefore be
checked against evidence. We write $p$ for the complete task context, including
the prompt, applicable system instructions, dialogue history, domain
constraints, attached data, and any designated tool or environment state. An
\emph{evaluator}, which may be a human annotator, an automated pipeline, or
both, identifies the truth-conditional commitments made in the response.

Evaluating a claim then takes two distinct steps. First, claim verification
assigns one of three \emph{evidence states} relative to the standard the task
uses to settle claims, which we call its \emph{task oracle}:
\textsc{entailed}, \textsc{contradicted}, or \textsc{unknown}. Here,
\textsc{unknown} means only that the task oracle neither entails nor
contradicts the claim; it is not a statement about all possible knowledge. Second, the framework assigns one of three \emph{claim
labels}: grounded, hallucination, or licensed divergence. Entailment and
contradiction settle the label directly; only an \textsc{unknown} claim proceeds
to the permission-scope and required-status-marking checks.

We write the contract as $\TC=(O,\Gamma,\mu)$. The task oracle $O$ settles the
evidence state, the permission scope $\Gamma$ specifies which evidence-unknown
content the task authorizes, and the required status marking $\mu$ specifies
how that content must be presented.
\FloatBarrier

The response's \emph{observed status
marking} records whether it presents a claim as established, hedged, or
explicitly hypothetical. The evaluator compares this marking with $\mu$. When
a claim violates the contract, a \emph{reason code} records which condition
failed.

An \textsc{entailed} claim receives the grounded label (\Grd). A
\textsc{contradicted} claim receives the hallucination label (\Hall) with
reason code \textsc{contradicted}. An \textsc{unknown} claim receives the
licensed-divergence label (\LD) only when it lies inside $\Gamma$ and its
observed status marking satisfies $\mu$; otherwise it receives \Hall{} with
reason code \textsc{out-of-scope} or \textsc{marking-failure}.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} defines the normalized claim representation used
for these comparisons.

Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-to-claim} illustrates the \textsc{unknown} branch;
Section~\ref{sec:framework} specifies the complete rule.

The framework evaluates individual truth-conditional claims.
It does not by itself verify global logical validity, cross-claim coherence,
response completeness, or answer relevance.
It nevertheless supports a response-level record that combines the
claim trace with how much of the response the evaluator managed to recover and
with any breakdown in the evaluation procedure itself.
Style-marking alignment and any task-specific creativity criterion are reported
beside that record rather than collapsed into it, and neither ever changes a
claim label.

\paragraph{Contributions}
This position paper makes three contributions.
First, it introduces the truth contract $\TC=(O,\Gamma,\mu)$ and a
claim-labeling rule that separates the task oracle, permission scope, and
required status marking.
Second, it derives a response-level record while reporting
style-marking alignment and task-specific creative success separately.
Third, it uses a purposive resource mapping and worked cases to identify missing
evaluation combinations and to derive a falsifiable research agenda.

\paragraph{Truth contracts versus separate factuality and instruction-following judgments}
Evidence-based factuality and source-faithfulness methods ask whether a claim
is supported by a designated reference, whereas instruction-following and
intent-aware evaluation ask whether the response satisfies or correctly
interprets task constraints
\citep{maynez_faithfulness_2020,min_factscore_2023,zhou_ifeval_2023,hao_faithqa_2025}.
These judgments are complementary and may require explicit reconciliation
\citep{wu_dancing_2024}, but neither output alone decides whether an
\textsc{unknown} claim receives the hallucination or licensed-divergence label.
A rubric can reproduce those labels only by representing $O$, $\Gamma$, and
$\mu$ and applying the same claim-level decision rule; in that case, it
instantiates the truth-contract rule.

\paragraph{A secondary problem: claim recovery under marked style}
Applying the rule presupposes that the evaluator recovers the right claims in
the first place. Marked wording may make recovery harder, but it remains outside
the truth contract; Section~\ref{subsec:style} defines the variable and
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics} gives the stability test.


\paragraph{Mapping and worked cases}
The purposive mapping codes forty-one resources for the three contract fields
and claim-preserving style variation. It identifies patterns and missing
combinations within the selected sample, not their prevalence in the wider
literature. Five worked cases then state what is fixed, what changes, and which
change in claim recovery or label the rule predicts. They are specification
checks rather than an annotation study; Section~\ref{sec:agenda} turns that
limitation into empirical questions.

\paragraph{Organization}
Sections~\ref{sec:framework} and~\ref{sec:related} define the framework and
position it against neighboring evaluation paradigms.
Sections~\ref{sec:methodology}--\ref{sec:conclusion} present the mapping,
worked cases, research agenda, discussion, and conclusion. The appendices
specify the response-level diagnostics, publish the resource coding, and report
the leaderboard measurements cited in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

\section{Framework}\label{sec:framework}

The position stated above assigns three different roles to evidence,
authorization, and presentation.
Those roles are usable only if each is specified precisely enough for two
evaluators to apply them to the same response.
This section gives that specification.

\subsection{Framework Overview}

Consider the running example used throughout this paper: the Rosetta Stone
museum label of Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-to-claim}.
The task context supplies the frozen Wikipedia excerpt as the task oracle, asks
for a museum label whose historical statements stay faithful to it, and permits
one plausible reconstruction provided it is marked as conjecture.
Suppose the response contains the span ``One possible reconstruction places the
complete stele near a temple entrance.''
The evaluator first interprets the span in this task context and recovers one
contextualized claim: the complete stele stood near a temple entrance.
It then canonicalizes that claim into a normalized content component $q^*$ and
an observed status marking component $m^*$. The first records entities,
predicates, negation, quantities, and temporal scope; the second records
epistemic modality and relevant discourse framing.
Here that framing is the fact that the claim is offered as a possibility rather
than asserted.
The evaluator performs claim verification by comparing $q^*$ with the task
oracle $O$: the excerpt neither entails nor contradicts the placement, so the
evidence state is \textsc{unknown}.
The \textsc{unknown} claim then proceeds to the remaining contract fields. It
falls inside the permission scope, which allows one marked reconstruction, and
$m^*$ satisfies the required status marking, which demands that a conjecture be
stated as such. The claim therefore receives the licensed-divergence label
rather than the hallucination label.
Had the same span appeared under the strict-grounding task of
Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-to-claim}, whose permission scope is empty, it would have
received the hallucination label with reason code \textsc{out-of-scope}: the
evidence state would have been identical and the label different.
The evaluator separately compares requested and observed style-marking levels;
that comparison does not create or excuse a truth-conditional claim.

Claim labeling has two phases. The first prepares a claim; the second decides
its label, and branches exactly once.

\begin{description}
  \item[Preparation.] The evaluator selects a \emph{response span}, that is, a
        contiguous stretch of the response treated as one unit of
        interpretation, typically a clause or a sentence.
        It then recovers the contextualized claims that the span commits to and
        canonicalizes each one into normalized content $q^*$ and observed
        status marking $m^*$.
  \item[Decision.] The evaluator records the evidence state of $q^*$ against
        $O$. If that state is \textsc{entailed} or \textsc{contradicted}, the
        label follows immediately and no further check applies. If it is
        \textsc{unknown}, two checks decide: whether $q^*$ falls inside
        $\Gamma$, and, for a claim that does, whether $m^*$ satisfies
        $\mu$. The evaluator then assigns the claim label and, for a
        violation, the reason code.
\end{description}

Evidence settles two labels directly; only the \textsc{unknown} case proceeds
to the remaining contract checks, $\Gamma$ and $\mu$.
If preparation or adjudication fails, the span remains unlabeled rather than
receiving a default label. The claim trace is the primary output;
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics} specifies the response-level record and the
procedural failures reported in place of a label.

\subsection{The Truth Contract and Task Oracle}\label{subsec:contract}

\paragraph{Truth contract}
A truth contract specifies what the task treats as established, what the
response may introduce beyond it, and how such content must be presented.
For a task context $p$, it is the triple $\TC=(O,\Gamma,\mu)$.
The three fields answer separate questions. What task-specific standard decides
the evidence state? What content may extend beyond what that standard
establishes? How must such content be marked?
Accordingly, the contract is indexed by the complete task context rather than
the literal prompt alone.

\paragraph{Task oracle ($O$)}
The task oracle is the standard a task uses to determine whether a claim is
entailed, contradicted, or unknown.
It specifies which information sources are admissible, their temporal scope,
their relative authority, and the procedure used to resolve conflicts.
Existing surveys use different notions of source, context, fact, and world
knowledge \citep{ji_survey_2023,bang_hallulens_2025}; reproducible evaluation
therefore requires a concrete task-oracle specification or an explicit
adjudication procedure.
A task oracle may comprise a source document, a retrieval set, a database,
gold labels, an environment or tool state, a human adjudication procedure, the
declared constraints of a fictional world, or a combination of these sources.
For example, a supplied article can instantiate the task oracle for a
source-faithfulness task, execution tests can instantiate it for a code task,
and declared story-world constraints can instantiate it for in-frame claims.

The task oracle does not represent whatever the user would like to be true.
It records what the evaluation treats as established or authoritative for the
task.
Content that the task permits beyond the oracle is represented separately by
the permission scope $\Gamma$.
An instruction to propose a hypothesis can therefore authorize the proposal
without adding that hypothesis to the task oracle or making it true.

The task oracle is idealized because it specifies the standard that the
evaluation should apply.
A concrete implementation may still fail to access, complete, or adjudicate
that standard reliably.
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics} records such implementation failures rather than
converting them into claim labels.

\paragraph{Permission scope ($\Gamma$)}
Return to the clinical assistant of Section~\ref{sec:intro}, now asked to list
candidate explanations of a patient's symptoms. The task lets the response
raise a possible diagnosis the record does not establish, and forbids it to
supply a drug dose the record does not contain. Both are claims the evidence
leaves unknown; only one is authorized.
$\Gamma$ records that difference. It specifies the topics, entities, claim
types, or fictional world in which evidence-unknown content may be introduced.
A factual-only task has an empty permission scope: every truth-conditional
claim must be entailed by the task oracle $O$.
Searle's account of fictional discourse provides indirect support for
distinguishing story-world commitments from assertions presented as
real-world fact \citep{searle_logical_1975}.
We operationalize that distinction through permission scope, which may
include invented characters and events while excluding claims about named
real people.

Permission scope records authorization, not value.
The check therefore asks what the task allows, not what is worth saying: a
carefully reasoned but invented drug dose still receives the hallucination
label, whereas an unhelpful but in-frame fictional invention still receives the
licensed-divergence label and a low score on whatever usefulness criterion the
task defines.
Section~\ref{subsec:creativity} situates this separation against creativity
evaluation, which scores value on its own terms.

\paragraph{Required status marking ($\mu$)}
$\mu$ specifies how the response must signal an evidence-unknown claim.
The signal may be a local hedge, a hypothesis label, a section heading, or a
task-level fictional frame.
In the clinical task above, ``this may be early Lyme disease, but serology is
required'' satisfies the required status marking, while ``this is early Lyme
disease'' does not.
The observed status marking $m^*$ is recovered from the complete response and
task context because the relevant marker may appear outside the local span.
The evaluator then compares $m^*$ with the required status marking to decide
whether the claim is marked as $\mu$ requires.

\subsection{Claim Recovery and Canonicalization}\label{subsec:unit}

We use a claim-level unit because long responses may contain multiple checkable
propositions alongside material that is not independently verifiable
\citep{min_factscore_2023,song_veriscore_2024}.
When a response span contains a commitment, interpreting it within the
complete response and task context yields one or more contextualized
truth-conditional claims.
The same wording can therefore yield different claims in fiction, metaphor, and hypothetical language.
We reserve two terms for two different operations.
\emph{Claim extraction} refers to existing methods that segment a response into
checkable units; Section~\ref{subsec:extraction} reviews them.
\emph{Claim recovery} refers to the broader, context-sensitive step that
attributes claims to the response before canonicalization, and it is the step
this rule relies on.

Before claim verification, the evaluator canonicalizes each recovered claim into
two components, $q^*$ and $m^*$.
The component $q^*$ records normalized truth-conditional content, including entities, predicates, negation, quantities, temporal scope, and any attribution that changes what is claimed.
The component $m^*$ records observed status marking, including relevant
modality and fictional or hypothetical framing.
This separation allows $q^*$ to be checked against the task oracle and
permission scope without discarding the presentation information needed by
$\mu$.

Claim recovery follows three rules.
First, commitments that can receive different evidence states are recovered
as separate claims.
Second, attribution, negation, modality, temporal scope, and discourse frame
are preserved in $q^*$ or $m^*$ as appropriate.
Third, metaphorical wording does not create a separate claim unless the
response makes an attributable truth-conditional commitment.

Claim recovery can fail in two directions.
\emph{Under-decomposition} merges commitments that could receive different
evidence states; \emph{over-decomposition} splits one commitment into several
redundant subclaims.
Over-decomposition distorts claim counts, coverage estimates, and any
response-level aggregation based on label frequency: for an \textsc{unknown}
permitted commitment, it inflates the number of \LD-labeled claims without
adding new content.
Evaluation should therefore measure both directions against an independently
adjudicated claim set.

Some spans yield no truth-conditional claim at all.
Such a span receives no claim label, and the absence of a label is a normal
outcome rather than one of the failures defined in
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics}.
Figurative language is interpreted in the complete response and task context rather than literalized automatically.
Every truth-conditional commitment recovered from the span is canonicalized
before claim verification.

We use \emph{claim verification} for the step that compares the canonical
claim content $q^*$ with the task oracle $O$ and, when adjudication succeeds,
assigns exactly one evidence state: \textsc{entailed},
\textsc{contradicted}, or \textsc{unknown}.
Claim verification therefore follows claim recovery and canonicalization but
precedes the permission-scope and required-status-marking checks.

A pair of matched realizations instantiates \emph{claim-preserving style
variation} when both yield the same canonical claims under a fixed task oracle
$O$, permission scope $\Gamma$, and required status marking $\mu$.
Only their surface realization and requested or observed style-marking levels
may differ.
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics} gives the formal stability test.

Recovering each claim correctly does not establish that the response combines
those claims through a valid inference.
Claim recovery and global response verification are separate evaluation
problems, and the scope limit stated in Section~\ref{sec:intro} applies here:
a response whose claims are individually well labeled may still reason badly
from them.

\subsection{From Evidence State to Claim Label}\label{subsec:rule}

Once a stable canonical claim has been obtained and the three contract fields
are fixed, the evaluator attempts claim verification against $O$.
When adjudication succeeds, claim labeling applies the rule below.
That rule is not uniform across evidence states.
For an \textsc{entailed} or a \textsc{contradicted} claim, the evidence state
alone determines the label.
For an \textsc{unknown} claim it does not.
The permission-scope and required-status-marking checks then decide.
We reserve \textsc{entailed}, \textsc{contradicted}, and \textsc{unknown} for
evidence states.
The three claim labels are grounded, hallucination, and licensed divergence.
We abbreviate them as \Grd, \Hall, and \LD\ in the rule below, in figures, and
in tables, and use the full names in running text.

The two vocabularies are not redundant.
An evidence state describes the relation between a claim and the task oracle; a
claim label describes the standing of that claim under the task.
Two of the three evidence states map onto a label one to one, but the
hallucination label does not map back: it is reached from
\textsc{contradicted}, and also from \textsc{unknown} by two different routes.
That is why a claim label carries a reason code and an evidence state does not.
We write \emph{grounded} rather than \emph{supported} for this label because
fact-verification resources such as FEVER \citep{thorne_fever_2018} use
\textsc{supported} for an evidence state, namely for what we call
\textsc{entailed}.
Reusing that word across the two vocabularies would blur the very distinction
the rule depends on.

For each canonical claim, the evaluator first records whether $O$ entails,
contradicts, or leaves $q^*$ unknown.
We call a claim with evidence state \textsc{unknown} an
\emph{\textup{\textsc{unknown}} claim}.

The claim label then follows three rules:
\begin{description}
  \item[\Grd.] The task oracle entails $q^*$.
  \item[\Hall.] The task oracle contradicts $q^*$, or it leaves $q^*$
        unknown and the claim falls outside $\Gamma$ or fails the required
        status marking $\mu$. The evaluator records exactly one reason code,
        applying the following precedence:
        \begin{enumerate}
          \item if $O$ contradicts $q^*$, record \textsc{contradicted};
          \item otherwise, if the evidence state is \textsc{unknown} and $q^*$
                falls outside $\Gamma$, record \textsc{out-of-scope};
          \item otherwise, if $q^*$ lies inside $\Gamma$ but $m^*$ fails
                $\mu$, record \textsc{marking-failure}.
        \end{enumerate}
  \item[\LD.] The task oracle leaves $q^*$ unknown, the claim lies
        inside $\Gamma$, and $m^*$ satisfies $\mu$.
\end{description}
The licensed-divergence label records contract compliance and authorization
under the task, not established truth, usefulness, overall response quality,
or safety. A contradicted claim
therefore receives the hallucination label once the procedural prerequisites
are satisfied. In fiction or counterfactual
tasks, the declared frame is part of the context used to recover the claim and
specify $O$; an in-frame invention is not automatically a real-world
assertion. An entailed claim that violates a separate style instruction remains
grounded, while the style or instruction-following evaluation records the
mismatch.

The hallucination label is the single top-level label for every truth-contract
violation. The kind of violation is recorded by the mandatory reason code, not
by a separate label.

A claim that lies inside the permission scope but fails the required status
marking therefore receives the hallucination label with reason code
\textsc{marking-failure}. The task authorized the content only under a marking
the response did not supply. Presented as established rather than as a
hypothesis, the claim asserts more than the contract permits.

Splitting these cases into separate top-level labels would obscure that all
three are failures of the same contract, whereas the reason code preserves the
distinction for diagnosis and mitigation.
The reason code identifies the first contract condition that fails, so a claim
that is both outside $\Gamma$ and asserted without the required hedge
receives \textsc{out-of-scope}: the marking check applies only to claims the
task permits.

An explicit refusal or a statement such as ``the available evidence is
insufficient'' is evaluated as a meta-claim about the evidence. It is not
automatically treated as asserting the embedded proposition. Treating the
refusal or insufficiency statement as a meta-claim prevents calibrated
abstention from being mislabeled as hallucination.

\subsection{Style as a Separate Robustness Variable}\label{subsec:style}

Style-marking level is a separate robustness variable: it may affect claim
recovery, but it never changes truth, evidence state, permission scope, or a
claim label. Required status marking concerns the epistemic or discourse status
of an \textsc{unknown} claim; style marking concerns rhetorical form.

By \emph{style marking} we mean the degree to which the wording of a response
departs from plain expository prose.
Plain prose is minimally marked; sustained metaphor or a strong authorial voice
is heavily marked, and stylistics describes such departures as foregrounding
\citep{leech_short_style_1981,vanpeer_stylistics_2020}.
The departure may be lexical, syntactic, phonological, or discourse-level, and
it may be produced by devices such as deviation, parallelism, repetition, or
metaphor.
Style marking is a matter of degree; it is not the identity of a named style,
and it is not evidence of factual error.
Case~E in Section~\ref{sec:cases} gives a minimal pair: two realizations of one
canonical claim, one minimally marked and one marked.

The requested style-marking level records the degree of style marking the task
asks for. It is a separate task variable available as contextual input during
claim recovery, and it takes one of three ordered values:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{minimally marked}: plain expository prose, with minimal rhetorical transformation and no salient authorial voice;
  \item \emph{locally marked}: local metaphor, register shift, or a light stylistic voice without sustained transformation of the response;
  \item \emph{sustained marked style}: sustained figurative language, a salient voice, or substantial stylistic transformation.
\end{itemize}
The levels rank only the degree of style marking. They do not represent the
identity of a named style, creativity, factuality, or permission scope.
Where a study needs to index the three values, we write
$\sigma\in\{0,1,2\}$; Section~\ref{sec:agenda} and
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics} use that indexing to state factorial designs.

Because style is multi-dimensional, the three levels form a working
discretization rather than a validated universal scale
\citep{kang_style_2021,jin_deep_2022}.
The boundaries, especially between levels 1 and 2, require empirical
validation.

The requested level is specified by the task; the observed level is realized
by the response. Their \emph{prompt--response style-marking alignment} is
\textsc{match}, \textsc{mismatch}, or \textsc{not applicable} when the task
specifies no target. This provisional categorical rule may later be replaced
by ordinal distance or tolerance. If either level cannot be adjudicated
reliably, the component is withheld and a procedural failure is reported.
Style-marking alignment is neither a claim label nor a creativity score or
measure of model alignment.

Requested style-marking level is available as contextual input during claim
recovery, and its effect is tested rather than assumed.
Every recovered claim must remain warranted by linguistic evidence in the
produced response, and the observed level records the marking actually
produced.

Existing evaluators use claim decomposition, natural-language inference, or
semantic similarity, while model-based judges may prioritize style or apply a
narrower subset of human criteria
\citep{min_factscore_2023,chen_menli_2023,aynetdinov_semscore_2024,feuer_style_2025,lyu_converge_2026}.
The cited work does not hold a canonical claim fixed while wording varies;
whether current pipelines recover the same claims across matched style variants
remains an open empirical question.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

Each neighboring tradition reviewed below supplies one component of the
decision, and none supplies the decision itself.
Factuality and source-faithfulness evaluation define relations between claims
and their sources.
Claim-level evaluation supplies the unit of analysis.
Instruction-following evaluation represents broader task constraints.
Creativity research supplies separate criteria for novelty, usefulness, and
response quality.
The truth contract addresses what follows when the task oracle leaves a claim
\textsc{unknown}: whether permission scope and required status marking yield
the licensed-divergence label or the hallucination label.

\subsection{Hallucination Taxonomies}

Hallucination surveys organize the phenomenon along several related but
non-equivalent axes.
\citet{ji_survey_2023} distinguish intrinsic hallucinations, which contradict the source, from extrinsic hallucinations, which cannot be verified from it.
\citet{huang_survey_2025} separate factuality hallucinations, which involve factual contradiction or fabrication, from faithfulness hallucinations, which involve instruction, context, or logical inconsistency.
\citet{zhang_sirens_2025} instead distinguish input-, context-, and fact-conflicting outputs.
Other reviews organize causes across the model lifecycle or examine factuality and faithfulness metrics for open-ended generation \citep{wang_factuality_survey_2024,malin_faithfulness_review_2025,lamba_lifecycle_2026}.
From a creativity perspective, \citet{jiang_survey_2024} distinguish divergent generation from convergent evaluation.

These surveys classify mismatches by their evidence relation, inconsistency type, source, cause, or evaluation setting.
The truth contract addresses a subsequent decision: whether a claim that the
task oracle leaves \textsc{unknown} falls inside $\Gamma$ and satisfies
$\mu$.
The resulting claim label should therefore remain distinct from the
descriptive taxonomy.

\subsection{Task Oracles in Factuality and Faithfulness Evaluation}

Source-faithfulness evaluation asks whether a claim is supported by a
supplied source document, whereas factuality evaluation checks it against
external knowledge \citep{maynez_faithfulness_2020,kryscinski_evaluating_2020}.
In our framework, the supplied source document instantiates the task oracle in
the first setting; external knowledge sources or human adjudication may
instantiate it in the second. These task-oracle choices determine whether the
claim is entailed, contradicted, or unknown. Permission scope and required
status marking then determine how an \textsc{unknown} claim is labeled.

\subsection{Claim Extraction, Recovery, and Commitment}\label{subsec:extraction}

Specifying the task oracle does not yet determine which propositions the
response commits to. Evaluation must first recover claims in context.
Assertion is treated here as a public commitment to propositional content
\citep{stalnaker_assertion_1978}.
Because interpretation depends on conversational context, claim recovery
retains the context needed to attribute that commitment
\citep{clark_using_1996}.
Canonicalization may add context needed to make a claim stand alone, but it
must preserve the original meaning \citep{choi_decontextualization_2021}.
Hedges can qualify categorical commitment \citep{hyland_hedging_1996}; their
observed contribution is recorded in $m^*$.
These sources motivate contextualized claims and canonicalization; they do not
validate the proposed categories of required status marking.

Operationalizing contextual commitment requires a reliable unit of
verification, which motivates claim extraction and recovery.
External claim-extraction methods decompose responses into checkable units
before verification
\citep{min_factscore_2023,song_veriscore_2024,metropolitansky_claimify_2025}.
Some verification pipelines further adapt verification effort to claim-level
uncertainty rather than applying one policy to every claim
\citep{hassan_factor_2026}; our rule instead varies the label itself with
permission scope, independently of such uncertainty-based effort allocation.
Coverage errors omit attributable commitments, whereas atomicity errors merge
propositions that could receive different evidence states
\citep{metropolitansky_claimify_2025,wanner_closer_2024}.
Decontextualization can remove attribution, modality, or discourse framing,
and canonicalization can alter $q^*$ or $m^*$
\citep{wanner_closer_2024,choi_decontextualization_2021}.
These methods supply claim-extraction machinery, but claim recovery and
canonicalization, as distinguished in Section~\ref{subsec:unit}, are evaluated
separately before claim labels are assigned.

\subsection{Instruction Following, Permission, and Status Marking}

Claim extraction settles what is being evaluated, but not what the task
allowed. That second question belongs to instruction-following and intent-aware
evaluation, and it is the paradigm closest to ours: a reader may reasonably ask
why the truth contract is not simply a well-specified instruction-following
rubric. This subsection states what that literature measures and where the
claim-labeling decision falls outside it.

Instruction-following evaluation asks whether a response satisfies the
constraints the task expresses, and its benchmarks turn those constraints into
automatically checkable requirements on the output
\citep{zhou_ifeval_2023}.
More recent frameworks decompose a constraint along several dimensions rather
than treating constraint type as the only variable
\citep{ye_muldimif_2025}.
Intent-aware evaluation adds a diagnosis of query conditions the response
omitted or misread \citep{hao_faithqa_2025}.
Work that studies instruction following and source faithfulness jointly finds
that the two objectives can pull apart and require explicit reconciliation
\citep{wu_dancing_2024}.
Together, this literature supplies what the truth contract presupposes but does
not itself provide: a way to read task constraints off a task context reliably
enough to act on them.

Two properties of that literature bear on our proposal. First, a constraint in
these benchmarks is a requirement on the response, checked after generation. A
permission scope is not a requirement: it authorizes content without demanding
it, and a response that adds nothing evidence-unknown violates no permission.
An instruction-following rubric can therefore express $\mu$, which does
constrain how content is presented, more naturally than $\Gamma$.
Second, judging at constraint level is not the same as judging the response as
a whole. \citet{lee_mcjudgebench_2026} show that LLM judges are usually assessed
only through overall-response verdicts, and evaluate them instead against
per-constraint labels. That distinction mirrors the one this paper draws between
a claim label and a response-level record, and it arises for the same reason:
an aggregate judgment hides which requirement produced it.

The relation to our rule is therefore one of instantiation rather than
competition. As Section~\ref{sec:intro} states, a rubric detailed enough to
represent the task oracle, permission scope, and required status marking
explicitly, and to apply them in the order the rule specifies, would reproduce
its labels. It would do so by implementing the rule, not by replacing it. What
the rule contributes is the claim that these three fields are the ones a
hallucination judgment needs, and that the third case is decided by
authorization rather than by evidence.

Two boundaries follow. Permission scope is not a measure of usefulness, and
required status marking is not a general stylistic score. Safety, legal, and
domain constraints bound $\Gamma$ from outside and remain separate
assessments; a task context cannot widen the permission scope past them.

\subsection{Creativity and Response-Level Quality}\label{subsec:creativity}

The previous paradigms ask what a task requires. Creativity research asks
something the truth contract deliberately does not encode: whether the content
is any good. This subsection explains why we keep the two apart.

Creativity research has long defined a creative product as one that is both
novel and useful or appropriate \citep{runco_standard_2012}.
The two criteria are not interchangeable and are not equally weighted.
\citet{diedrich_are_2015} report that usefulness predicts rated creativity only
within highly novel ideas, making novelty a first-order and usefulness a
second-order criterion.
\citet{pichot_reflective_2024} find the two components weakly or negatively
correlated in judged ideas and argue that collapsing them into one composite
score therefore misrepresents what the standard definition asserts.
Value is also the dimension on which automatic judges are least reliable:
LLM creativity evaluators converge with human raters on novelty and diverge on
the contextual criteria that carry value \citep{lyu_converge_2026}.
We take from this literature the practice of keeping novelty, usefulness, and
quality as separately reported criteria, and the caution that a single
creativity score hides which criterion produced it.

A parallel separation appears on the factuality side. Instruction tuning that
makes responses more informative can reduce their truthfulness
\citep{wu_balancing_2025}, and preference learning can reward fluent, helpful
wording over accurate content, so recent work treats the
factuality--informativeness balance as a controllable trade-off rather than a
single objective \citep{gong_factuality_2026}.
Usefulness and factual support therefore come apart empirically, not only
conceptually.

Neither literature, however, supplies the decision our rule makes. Value is a
property of the content that an evaluator scores after the fact; permission
scope is a property of the task that must be fixed before the response is read.
Section~\ref{subsec:contract} states that ordering, and the minimal pair given
there shows why it matters: an ingenious fabrication remains a hallucination
and a dull in-frame invention remains licensed divergence.
Recent work that distinguishes potentially valuable or ``intelligent''
hallucinations from defective ones therefore answers a different question from
ours \citep{jiang_survey_2024,sui_confabulation_2024,yang_hicbench_2025},
as do bounded uses of confabulation for archival gap-filling
\citep{sui_critical_2025} and composite metrics that try to separate pedagogical
narrative reformulation from factual error in AI-generated science stories
\citep{argese_storyscore_2026}.
Those proposals grade divergence by its worth; the truth contract asks first
whether the task authorized it at all.

Creativity and overall response quality accordingly require task-specific
criteria beyond claim labels. Novelty, usefulness, preference, and quality may
contribute to a creative-task assessment, but none of them makes an unsupported
proposition true or expands permission scope. A response can therefore satisfy
the truth contract and still perform poorly on the task's creativity or quality
rubric; conversely, a highly rated response can still contain a
contract-violating claim. These diagnostics should remain visible rather than
being collapsed into the claim label.

\subsection{Global Reasoning and Complete-Response Evaluation}\label{subsec:global}

The paradigms above evaluate claims one at a time, and so does our rule. A
separate body of work asks what that unit misses once the claims are put back
together. It is the literature that bounds our scope, and we review it here for
that reason.

The limitation has been named directly. \citet{yan_logicscore_2026} call it
\emph{attribution myopia}: evaluation verifies isolated statements and their
attributions while overlooking the global logical integrity of a long-form
answer, so that models produce responses which are factually grounded yet
logically incoherent. That description applies to a claim trace as much as to
an attribution score. Three failure modes have been studied separately.

First, individually acceptable claims may be joined by reasoning that does not
hold. Work on chain-of-thought faithfulness shows that a model's stated
reasoning need not reflect the process that produced its answer, so a correct
final claim is no evidence that the steps leading to it were sound
\citep{arcuschin_cot_2025}. Step-level benchmarks make the same point from the
evaluation side: methods that detect problems at the response level cannot say
where in a chain the failure occurred or what kind it was
\citep{pham_grace_2026}. Our claim trace localizes a violation to a claim and a
contract field, which is a finer unit than a response-level score but still not
a step of an inference.

Second, claims may be individually adjudicable and mutually inconsistent.
\citet{mundler_selfcontradictory_2024} show that a model can assert two
contradictory sentences within one context, and that detecting this requires
comparing generated statements with each other rather than each with a source.
A truth contract compares every claim with $O$ and never compares claims with
one another, so a response whose claims are each entailed can still contradict
itself without any claim receiving \Hall.

Third, a response may be accurate and incomplete. Long-form factuality
evaluation has largely measured precision over extracted claims, and recent work
argues that recall matters as much: \citet{liu_verifact_2025} report that high
precision does not imply high coverage of the facts a response should contain,
and \citet{jafari_recall_2026} weight recall by the importance of the omitted
material. Claim-recovery coverage, defined in
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics}, measures whether the evaluator found the claims
a response \emph{made}; it does not measure whether the response made the claims
the task \emph{required}.

Evaluation of the complete response must compose the claim trace with separate
checks for reasoning, coherence, coverage, relevance, and task-specific
success; a favorable response-level judgment cannot retroactively authorize a
claim that violates the truth contract.
Section~\ref{sec:agenda} states this composition as an open research problem
rather than a solved one.

\subsection{Claim-Preserving Style Variation}
The paradigms reviewed so far concern what a claim commits to and what a task
permits. A last body of work concerns how the same commitment can be worded in
different ways.
Stylistics and style-transfer research examine changes in linguistic form
intended to preserve content
\citep{leech_short_style_1981,vanpeer_stylistics_2020,pavlick_empirical_2016,rao_dear_2018,briakou_evaluating_2021}.
That literature measures whether content survives a change of form, but it does
not ask whether an evaluator still recovers the same claims from the changed
form.
Hallucination surveys and benchmarks, by contrast, primarily organize evidence
relations such as support, contradiction, or unverifiability
\citep{ji_survey_2023,huang_survey_2025,bang_hallulens_2025}.
They hold form constant and vary the evidence relation, which is the mirror
image of the style-transfer setting.
Neither literature therefore tests claim recovery across matched realizations
of one canonical claim.
That gap motivates the controlled test defined in
Section~\ref{sec:framework} and the resource analysis reported below.

\section{Resource Mapping}\label{sec:methodology}

Section~\ref{sec:related} argued that the traditions reviewed do not make the
task oracle, permission scope, and required status marking separately
available. We now test that diagnosis across the selected resources.
We code forty-one evaluation resources for whether their designs represent each
of the three contract fields, and for whether any of them holds the wording of
a claim fixed while those fields vary.
The coding is descriptive; the next subsection states what it can and cannot
support before the results are reported.

\subsection{Resource Selection and Scope}

The unit of analysis in the purposive mapping is the evaluation resource.
We use \emph{evaluation resource} as an umbrella term for a benchmark,
dataset, or evaluation protocol with an identifiable evaluation design;
FEVER enters the mapping as a benchmark and dataset, and WritingBench as a
benchmark with an associated evaluation protocol.
We purposively selected forty-one such resources so that the sample spans the
design space the argument concerns: factual question answering, grounded
generation, intent evaluation, creative writing, divergent thinking, and
constrained problem solving.
Purposive selection lets us search deliberately across the design space
relevant to the argument. It can identify combinations absent from the
selected resources, but not establish that they never occur elsewhere or
estimate their prevalence.

Each resource was coded by the authors against the closed vocabulary reported
in Appendix~\ref{app:mapping}, and the resource-level coding is published in

underlying evidence.
The mapping is accordingly a purposive state-of-the-art diagnosis: it records
what the selected evaluation designs do and do not represent and motivates
the missing combination targeted by the framework. It does not directly
validate the framework, demonstrate a performance improvement, or estimate
prevalence in the wider literature.

\subsection{Coding Protocol}

For each resource, we recorded seven questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item What task-oracle type $O$, if any, is available for adjudicating claim
        support?
  \item What quality, preference, novelty, or task-success criterion, if any,
        is used separately from the task oracle?
  \item Does the protocol control or score requested style-marking level
        $\sigma$ while holding target claim content fixed?
  \item Which nominal task profile best describes it: strict grounding, intent
        evaluation, declared-frame invention and ideation, constrained solution
        search, or heterogeneous suites and tool use?
  \item How does the protocol represent permission scope $\Gamma$?
  \item Does it score required status marking $\mu$ separately?
  \item Across such controlled variants, does the protocol compare recovered
        claims or claim labels?
\end{enumerate}

For task-oracle type, the closed coding vocabulary is source document,
retrieval set, database or knowledge base, gold labels, environment or tool
state, human adjudication, fictional-world constraints, mixed or
task-dependent, and none identifiable. The original resources do not
necessarily use the term \emph{task oracle}. The coding records our
interpretation of the adjudication standard available in each evaluation
design.

Question~4 uses a term that requires definition.
A \emph{nominal task profile} is a categorical label that we assign to each
resource to summarize the kind of task it evaluates.
Profiles are descriptive groupings introduced for this mapping.
They are not claimed by the original resources, they are not ordered, and they
never enter the claim-labeling rule.
FEVER, for example, is coded as strict grounding because its claims are
verified against Wikipedia, which instantiates its task oracle, and no
unattested content is authorized. LitBench is coded as declared-frame
invention and ideation because it evaluates stories written under a story
frame; its human-preference signal evaluates response quality rather than
claim truth. ToolQA is coded as heterogeneous suites and tool use because its
tasks draw on fixed external data sources and programmatically generated gold
answers, yielding a mixed or task-dependent oracle.

Appendix~\ref{app:mapping} reports the resource-level coding using a closed
vocabulary defined before the tables.
The task oracle is kept separate from quality, preference, novelty,
feasibility, correctness, and general task-success criteria. Such criteria are
not treated as $O$ unless the protocol uses them to establish or contradict the
claim being adjudicated.

\subsection{What the Coding Shows}\label{sec:mapping}

The complete coding of the selected sample appears in Appendix
Tables~\ref{tab:full-mapping-factual-a}--\ref{tab:full-mapping-creative}.
It records whether each field is separately represented in an evaluation
design, not whether the protocol manipulates that field experimentally.
We report the three contract fields in turn, then the treatment of requested
style-marking level.

\paragraph{Task oracle}
Thirty-five of the forty-one selected resources make an adjudication standard
available, and our coding assigns it a type: most often a mixed or
task-dependent combination (fifteen resources), a source document (eight), or
gold labels (six), with three environment or tool states and single instances
of a retrieval set, a structured knowledge base, and a specified human
procedure.
The remaining six are coded \emph{none identifiable}: their protocols judge a
response without any standard that could establish or contradict the claims it
makes, and all six belong to the declared-frame invention and ideation
profile.
In the selected sample, the task oracle is the one contract field already
represented separately. All six exceptions belong to the declared-frame
invention and ideation profile; those protocols do not specify a standard that
can establish or contradict their claims.
Quality, preference, novelty, feasibility, and correctness criteria are coded
separately throughout; the five constrained solution search resources, for
example, use feasibility, tests, or specifications as task-success criteria,
none of which can establish or contradict an evaluated claim.

\paragraph{Permission scope}
No selected resource declares a permission scope.
In every one of the forty-one cases our coding had to infer one from the task
rather than read it off the protocol, and the inferred values fall into three
groups.
All twenty-two resources of the strict grounding profile are coded
\emph{inferred empty}: the design admits no evidence-unknown content, but no
part of the protocol says so.
Eight declared-frame resources are coded \emph{implicit frame} and one
\emph{partial}: a story, a task, or an ideation frame plainly licenses
invention, yet its boundary is nowhere stated, so nothing in the design
separates an in-frame invention from an out-of-frame one.
The remaining ten are coded \emph{not separately represented}: the
intent-evaluation resource, the four heterogeneous suites, and the five
constrained solution search resources.
Every selected task has a permission scope, and no selected design declares it.

\paragraph{Required status marking}
No selected resource scores required status marking as a separate dimension.
Twenty-six are coded \emph{not scored}, and fifteen \emph{not separated},
meaning that a relevant marking criterion is absorbed into a broader task or
rubric rather than assessed on its own.
Nothing in the selected designs therefore distinguishes a permitted claim that
was hedged as the task required from the same claim asserted as established.
AA-Omniscience is the closest case and still falls outside: it explicitly
permits abstention, but folds abstention into its reliability metrics rather
than representing it as licensed evidence-unknown content
\citep{jackson_aaomniscience_2025}.

\paragraph{Requested style-marking level}
One resource, WritingBench \citep{wu_writingbench_2025}, scores a requested
style constraint; the other forty leave the requested style-marking level
uncontrolled.
No selected resource, including WritingBench, holds canonical claim content
fixed while manipulating that level.
Question~7 is therefore uniformly \emph{not applicable} in the selected sample
and is not repeated as a separate column in Appendix~\ref{app:mapping}.
General writing quality, creativity, or linguistic complexity is evaluated in
several creative-writing resources, but scoring those properties is not the
same as controlling the requested level with the claim held constant.
The selected factuality and faithfulness resources correspondingly do not test
whether claim-preserving style variation changes claim recovery or claim
labels; whether the selected verifiers exhibit this failure mode remains open.

\paragraph{The missing combination}
In our selected sample, no resource separately represents the task oracle,
permission scope, and required status marking while also controlling
claim-preserving style variation.
The absence is categorical rather than a matter of degree: it is not an empty
region on an ordinal scale, but a design that no selected protocol implements.

\paragraph{Case A: changing the task oracle}
In XSum, a summary statement may be absent from the source document while
remaining correct against external evidence \citep{maynez_faithfulness_2020}.
\textbf{Purpose.} To test that the claim label depends on which oracle the task
designates, not on the claim.
\par\textbf{Fixed.} The response span, canonical claim, and empty permission scope.
\par\textbf{Changed.} The source document and the external factuality standard
instantiate two different task oracles $O$.
\par\textbf{Predicted result.} The claim receives the hallucination label with
reason code \textsc{out-of-scope} when it is \textsc{unknown} relative to the
source-document task oracle under $\Gamma=\varnothing$, and the grounded
label when the external task oracle entails it.

\paragraph{Case B (multi-factor): a contextualization boundary across historical
and fictional frames}
This case is not a single-factor contrast: changing the discourse frame also
changes the task oracle, the permission scope, and the contextualized claim.
\textbf{Purpose.} To test that the recovered claim itself depends on the
discourse frame, so that identical strings need not express identical claims.
\par\textbf{Fixed.} The constructed string ``Caf\'e Verdier existed in 1920s Paris.''
\par\textbf{Changed.} The historical task treats a register with no Verdier entry as
exhaustive, whereas the fiction task uses declared story-world constraints;
the discourse frame, task oracle $O$, and permission scope $\Gamma$
therefore change.
Because the register is declared exhaustive for the establishments of the
period, its silence entails the negation rather than leaving the claim unknown;
a bounded but non-exhaustive oracle would have yielded \textsc{unknown}
instead, as in Figure~\ref{fig:prompt-to-claim}.
\par\textbf{Predicted result.} The historical task yields a real-world canonical
claim that receives the hallucination label with reason code
\textsc{contradicted}, whereas the fiction task yields an \textsc{unknown}
story-world canonical claim that receives the licensed-divergence label.
The comparison demonstrates that identical strings need not express identical
contextualized claims.

\paragraph{Case C: changing the required status marking}
This case restates the clinical pair of Section~\ref{subsec:contract} as a
controlled contrast: the task's permission scope allows a candidate diagnosis,
and its required status marking demands that an unconfirmed one be hedged.
\textbf{Purpose.} To test that permission alone is not enough: presentation is
part of the contract.
\par\textbf{Fixed.} The task oracle $O$, $\Gamma$, $\mu$, the proposition
that the patient has early Lyme disease, and its \textsc{unknown} evidence
state.
\par\textbf{Changed.} Only the observed status marking $m^*$.
\par\textbf{Predicted result.} ``This is early Lyme disease'' receives the
hallucination label with reason code \textsc{marking-failure}, whereas ``this may
be early Lyme disease, but serology is required'' receives the
licensed-divergence label. The proposition, the evidence state, and all three
contract fields are identical across the pair; only the wording that signals
epistemic status differs.

\paragraph{Case D: testing a scope boundary}
This case continues the fiction task of Case~B, where the declared story-world
frame already licenses invention.
\textbf{Purpose.} To test that a permission scope has a boundary, and that the
boundary is drawn around content rather than around the frame.
\par\textbf{Fixed.} The fiction task context of Case~B: its task oracle specifies
the story-world frame, its permission scope permits invention inside that frame,
and its frame-level required status marking is satisfied.
\par\textbf{Changed.} The claim concerns either the invented establishment or a
named real person presented as real history: ``Caf\'e Verdier stood on the rue
de Seine'' against ``Ernest Hemingway drank at Caf\'e Verdier.''
\par\textbf{Predicted result.} The in-frame claim about the invented
establishment can receive the licensed-divergence label. The claim about the
named real person asserts something about the actual world, falls outside
$\Gamma$, and receives the hallucination label with reason code
\textsc{out-of-scope}. The declared frame licenses invention within the story;
it does not license real-world attribution.

\paragraph{Case E: changing surface realization with a fixed claim}
This case does for a claim of Figure~\ref{fig:claim-label-annotation} what
Case~C does for the clinical pair.
\textbf{Purpose.} To test that wording, which lies outside the truth contract,
does not move the claim label.
\par\textbf{Fixed.} The task oracle $O$ of the Rosetta Stone example of
Figure~\ref{fig:claim-label-annotation}, $\Gamma$, $\mu$, and the
canonical claim $q^*$ that the decree appears in hieroglyphic, Demotic, and
Greek.
\par\textbf{Changed.} Only requested style-marking level $\sigma$ and surface
realization; the two responses are designed as a claim-preserving style
variation.
Neutral realization: ``The decree is written in hieroglyphic, Demotic, and
Greek.''
Marked realization: ``The same message speaks three times over: once in
hieroglyphs, once in Demotic, once in Greek.''
\par\textbf{Predicted result.} Both variants recover the same canonical claim and
receive the grounded label when the task oracle entails it; the observed
style-marking level and the style-marking alignment result are reported
separately.
A difference in recovered claims or labels would mean that the evaluator
failed this claim-preserving style-variation test.
It would not, by itself, establish that style generally changes claim recovery.

Together, the cases isolate changes in the task oracle, discourse frame,
required status marking, permission scope, and surface realization.
Their predictions define tests for the annotation and benchmark studies below.

\section{Research Agenda}\label{sec:agenda}

The following priorities define a validation sequence. Priorities~1--3 test
whether the truth contract can be annotated, manipulated, and used to diagnose
pipeline errors. Priorities~4 and~5 examine response-level evaluation and
mitigation, Priority~6 tests external validity, and Priority~7 extends the
framework to temporal updates and agentic action.
Table~\ref{tab:agenda} lists the open question for each priority; the prose
below gives the study designs, measures, and evidence that would weaken the
position.

Across Priorities~1--7, studies should compare contract-aware evaluation with a
combined factuality and instruction-following baseline, measuring claim-label
agreement, reason-code accuracy, and error localization.
\emph{Error localization} identifies the pipeline stage or truth-contract field
responsible for a disputed claim label.
To avoid circular evaluation, studies should use independently adjudicated gold
claim labels, apply the same claim-recovery procedure and task oracle to the
contract-aware method and its baseline, and specify decision thresholds before
evaluation.

\paragraph{1. Task-oracle and contract annotation}
The first unresolved question is whether evaluators can identify the task
oracle $O$, permission scope $\Gamma$, and required status marking $\mu$
consistently from the task context while annotating requested style-marking
level $\sigma$ separately.
Disagreement over a claim label may reflect incompatible readings of the task
oracle or contract rather than a substantive disagreement about the recovered
claim.
A public codebook should define contextualized claims, evidence states,
task-oracle type, admissible sources, temporal scope, authority ordering,
permission scope, required status marking, requested and observed
style-marking levels, and claim-preserving style variation.
Pilot studies should annotate each field separately, preserve genuine
disagreement, and report agreement by field and label, adjudication rate,
annotation time, and sources of disagreement.
Contract-inference studies should document how user requests, system
instructions, domain constraints, and safety or legal rules are reconciled,
and how clarifications update the contract in multi-turn interaction.
Because $O$, $\Gamma$, and $\mu$ are specified at task-context level,
their annotation cost amortizes across responses evaluated under the same task
context, whereas claim recovery, canonicalization, and claim verification
remain per-response costs.
\emph{Evidence against the position.} Persistent disagreement, or a cost out
of proportion to the gain, would weaken the position.

\paragraph{2. Factorial benchmark design}
Naturally occurring evaluation data often change the task oracle, permission
scope, required status marking, and surface realization together, preventing
attribution of a claim-label change to one field.
Without controlled contrasts, the proposed claim-labeling rule cannot be
distinguished from a combined factuality and instruction-following baseline.
A controlled factorial benchmark should therefore vary the task oracle
$O$, permission scope $\Gamma$, or required status marking $\mu$ while
holding the remaining fields and target claim fixed.
Separate claim-preserving style variation pairs should realize matched
canonical claims at
$\sigma\in\{0,1,2\}$ while keeping $O$, $\Gamma$, and $\mu$ fixed.
These contrasts test a hypothesis about claim recovery; they do not assume
that style changes the recovered claim set.
Evaluation should report claim-set equivalence, evidence state, claim label,
accuracy of observed style-marking level annotation, prompt--response
style-marking alignment,
and performance of the combined factuality and instruction-following baseline.
\emph{Evidence against the position.} Failure to preserve factual commitment
across controlled variants, or a combined factuality and instruction-following
baseline that matches contract-aware evaluation, would weaken the position.

\paragraph{3. Style-robust claim recovery and error localization}
An incorrect claim label may originate in span selection, claim recovery,
canonicalization, observed style-marking level annotation, claim verification
against the task oracle, or application of $\Gamma$ and $\mu$.
An end-to-end hallucination score cannot identify which stage produced the
error, so an apparent advantage may arise from a different upstream
claim recovery decision.
Claim recovery and verification pipelines should be tested on matched variants
at requested style-marking levels $0$, $1$, and $2$ containing metaphor,
irony, analogy, narrative voice, hypotheses, or declared fiction.
Style-marking level remains outside the truth contract in every condition.
For each response, the evaluation trace should record the recovered canonical
claims, evidence states, scope and marking decisions, claim labels, reason
codes, observed style-marking level, and prompt--response style-marking
alignment.
Comparisons among claim extraction, natural-language inference, retrieval, and
model-judge pipelines should report per-stage accuracy and calibration at
each level.
Measures should include omission, spurious-claim creation,
under-decomposition, over-decomposition, and resulting inflation or deflation of
\Grd{}, \Hall{}, and \LD{} counts.
The same claim recovery procedure should also be tested with and without
access to the requested style-marking level while the response and the three
contract fields remain fixed.
\emph{Evidence against the position.} No consequential diagnostic gain over
end-to-end evaluation would weaken the position.

\paragraph{4. Response-level record, global reasoning, and aggregation}
Claim-level labels do not establish whether a complete response reasons
validly, remains coherent, covers the required information, or answers the
question.
A response may contain only grounded and licensed-divergence claims yet
combine them through an invalid inference, contradict itself, omit a necessary
step, or remain irrelevant to the task.
Studies should evaluate separately the claim labels, task-oracle adequacy,
claim-recovery coverage, global logical validity, cross-claim coherence,
completeness, relevance, style-marking alignment, and task-specific
creativity.
They should then compare explicit rules for assigning the structured content
verdicts \textsc{contract-compliant}, \textsc{contract-violating}, and
\textsc{indeterminate}, as well as any optional aggregation used for ranking.
Decision rules, severity policies, and task-specific thresholds should be
declared before evaluation, and sensitivity analyses should report whether
model rankings or substantive conclusions change across plausible rules.
\emph{Evidence against the position.} The claim trace alone predicting
independently adjudicated response-level correctness and creative-task success
would weaken the position.

\paragraph{5. Mitigation without indiscriminate suppression}
A mitigation method can lower a conventional hallucination rate by increasing
abstention, avoiding figurative language, or suppressing claims with evidence
state \textsc{unknown} indiscriminately.
Such a reduction would not show whether reliability improved or the model
merely became more conservative.
Retrieval, verification, abstention, and constrained decoding should be
evaluated on five distinct outcomes: reduction of \Hall-labeled claims,
preservation of \LD-labeled claims, preservation of \Grd-labeled claims,
style-marking alignment, and creative-task success when applicable.
The first three are claim-label outcomes; the last two are separate
response-level dimensions.
Because creative-text judgments vary across readers, creative-task scores
should report uncertainty
\citep{diedrich_are_2015,marco_reader_2025}.
Per-contract reporting or Pareto comparisons should expose trade-offs among
these outcomes.
\emph{Evidence against the position.} A method that improves only by
suppressing permitted divergence would weaken the position.

\paragraph{6. Generalization across domains and languages}
The contract fields and three-level style-marking scale may work in the
constructed examples yet fail when task-oracle types, adjudication standards,
hedging conventions, genres, or instruction hierarchies change.
\emph{Evidence against the position.} Systematic domain- or language-specific
disagreement would weaken the position and limit the claimed generality of the
framework.
The annotation and contrast protocols should therefore be tested in
high-stakes factual domains, education, journalism, scientific communication,
brainstorming, and fiction, reporting field-level agreement separately by
domain so that a drop can be attributed to a particular contract field rather
than to the domain as a whole.
Multilingual studies should examine whether style marking, epistemic hedges,
and genre conventions support comparable annotations across languages and
cultures; required status marking is the field most exposed here, since
languages differ in how they grammaticalize epistemic commitment, so agreement
on $\mu$ by language is the measure to report.

\paragraph{7. Temporal truth-contract updates and agentic action}
In multi-step interaction, the task oracle and applicable instructions may
change over time, while later actions may consume earlier claims.
A static truth contract cannot represent an update to $O_t$ or show how a
\textsc{marking-failure} propagates from a claim into an action.
Agentic studies should model a time-indexed task oracle $O_t$ that can
incorporate tool results, environment state, updated retrieval, and human
approval.
They should assign a subtask-specific truth contract to each subtask and trace
claim labels and reason codes into downstream actions.
Measures should include task-oracle update accuracy, cross-subtask error
propagation, and whether an action relies on a claim that failed required
status marking.
\emph{Evidence against the position.} Static evaluation performing equally
well on the action-level criteria would weaken the position.


Together, these studies test the framework from annotation reliability to
downstream action. Until such evidence is available, the implications of the
position must remain limited to the distinctions stated below.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What Is Relative and What Is Not}\label{subsec:relative}

The term \emph{relative} has a restricted evaluative meaning in this paper.
The truth of a proposition is not task-relative, but its classification as a
contract violation can be.
The task oracle determines whether the proposition is entailed, contradicted,
or unknown; permission scope and required status marking determine how an
unknown claim is labeled.
The truth contract therefore changes the evaluation label, not what is true.
A licensed-divergence label records permission and compliant presentation; it
does not establish the truth of the claim.

This distinction also limits what follows from theoretical lower bounds.
\citet{kalai_calibrated_2024} derive a statistical lower bound for
hallucination on certain arbitrary facts under a generative-model calibration
condition.
\citet{xu_hallucination_2024} prove an impossibility result for computable
models used as general problem solvers over computable ground-truth functions,
and \citet{banerjee_llms_2024} argue for structural non-zero hallucination risk
from computability and undecidability limits.
These results rely on different assumptions; none alone implies a constant
deployment error rate or precludes statistically negligible rates in a
specified setting \citep{suzuki_negligible_2025}.

Those limits describe what is unavoidable in principle. Deployed systems are
not at that boundary, and neither scale nor recency moves them towards it.
AA-Omniscience measures closed-book factual reliability by instructing models
to answer when confident and to abstain otherwise. It reports an Omniscience
Index, which subtracts incorrect from correct answers over all items, alongside
a benchmark-specific hallucination rate: the share of non-correct answers that
are confidently wrong rather than partial or declined
\citep{jackson_aaomniscience_2025}.
Its launch analysis found that contemporaneous overall capability did not
reliably predict a low hallucination rate, and that larger open-weight models
did not consistently hallucinate less; an August 2026 leaderboard snapshot
shows the same dispersion across disclosed parameter counts and release dates
(Appendix~\ref{app:omniscience}).
We draw one limited conclusion from this. Scale, general capability, and
recency are not sufficient proxies for factual reliability, so the question of
how an evaluation should treat evidence-unknown content does not dissolve as
models improve.

Nor do these limits imply that every claim left unknown by the task oracle
should be suppressed.
Such suppression would also remove permitted hypotheses and in-frame
invention.
Empirical and conceptual work reports possible trade-offs between hallucination
mitigation and creativity
\citep{banerjee_does_2025,sun_hallucinating_2024,he_shakespearean_2025},
including evidence that preference-alignment strategies which reduce hallucination can
also lower the output uncertainty associated with literary quality
\citep{sui_uncertainty_2026}.

\subsection{Response-Level Interpretation}

Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics} defines the ordered content rule and the two
components reported beside it. What that rule does \emph{not} settle is worth
stating separately, because each gap is a place where a reader could over-read
the output.

\textsc{Contract-compliant} is a weaker guarantee than it sounds. It does not
mean the response is true. A response containing only licensed-divergence
claims is contract-compliant, and every one of its claims may be false: the
label records that the task authorized the divergence and the response marked
it, nothing more. Read as a reliability score, a compliance rate would therefore
mislead in exactly the tasks the framework was built for.

\textsc{Indeterminate} is a finding, not a missing value. It reports that the
evaluation could not be completed reliably, and it is produced by the evaluation
apparatus rather than by the response. Benchmarks that discard indeterminate
items, or fold them into either of the other two verdicts, silently convert a
measurement failure into a claim about the model. Because
claim-recovery coverage requires an independently adjudicated set of
commitments, the rate of indeterminate verdicts is also a property of how well
the benchmark was built, and should be reported as such.

Severity sits outside the rule for a different reason than the two reported
components: it is not a competing dimension but a weight applied afterwards.
An invented drug dose in the clinical task and an incorrect date on the museum
label both receive the hallucination label although their consequences differ.
Severity may order violations for triage or mitigation.
It must not decide whether a violation occurred.
If it did, the label would depend on the evaluator's estimate of harm rather
than on the contract, and two evaluators with different risk models would
disagree about a matter of fact.

The practical consequence is that the response-level record should travel with
the claim trace. Any single number derived from it answers an additional,
task-dependent question, and comparisons based on one should report their
sensitivity to the aggregation policy chosen. The evaluation target remains text
that is grounded where the task requires grounding, transparent where divergence is
permitted, and aligned with the requested style-marking level.

\subsection{Limitations and Safeguards}

The proposal has seven immediate limitations.
First, the scope limit stated in Section~\ref{subsec:global} is a limitation
and not only a boundary: a favorable distribution of claim labels licenses no
conclusion about the response as a whole, and each of the properties reviewed
there needs its own test (Priority~4).

Second, an ambiguous or inconsistent task context may prevent stable
truth-contract specification.
Priority~1 turns this into an annotation question. The evaluator must state
the precedence of system and user instructions and
applicable safety, legal, and domain constraints; otherwise it records a
truth-contract specification failure and leaves the affected claim label
undefined.

Third, the task oracle is idealized as the adjudication standard that should be
applied, but a concrete implementation may be incomplete, outdated,
inaccessible, or internally conflicting.
The evaluator should record a task-oracle adequacy or adjudication failure
rather than automatically assigning the \textsc{unknown} evidence state
(Priority~1).

Fourth, span selection, claim recovery, and canonicalization require judgment.
Validation therefore requires independently adjudicated canonical claims and
separate reporting of procedural failures and claim-recovery coverage
(Priority~3).

Fifth, permission scope must be specified from the task context before claim
labeling and cannot be widened after inspecting a response to turn a
fabrication into licensed divergence.
User-specified permission scope also remains bounded from outside by applicable safety,
legal, and domain constraints.

Sixth, the three-level style-marking scale is not a validated universal model
of style, its effect on claim recovery remains a testable hypothesis
(Priorities~2 and~3), and style-marking alignment is not by itself a measure of
creativity.

Seventh, the purposive mapping reports our own coding of the selected
resources and is scoped to them by design.
Independent recoding and a prevalence estimate over the broader literature are
natural extensions of the instrument, and the published resource-level coding
in Appendix~\ref{app:mapping} is what makes both extensions straightforward.

The framework is a claim-centered evaluation procedure with a structured
response-level interpretation; it is not a universal measure of truth,
reasoning, creativity, usefulness, or safety.


\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

We have argued that hallucination is relative to a task's truth contract.
This relativity concerns the evaluation label, not the truth value of the
claim: the contract changes the label, not what is true.

The truth contract makes explicit the task oracle, permission scope, and
required status marking.
An entailed claim receives the grounded label and a contradicted claim
receives the hallucination label.
An unknown claim receives the licensed-divergence label only when the task
permits it and the response marks it as required; otherwise it receives the
hallucination label with a reason code.

The framework evaluates claims individually, while a structured
response-level record combines the claim trace with task-oracle adequacy,
claim-recovery coverage, and procedural failures.
Global reasoning, completeness, and relevance require separate evaluation.
Style-marking alignment and task-specific creativity criteria also remain
separate dimensions and never change a claim label.
Usefulness and severity are likewise separate diagnostics: they may weight a
violation after labeling, but they do not determine whether one occurred.

In our selected sample, no resource separately represents the task oracle,
permission scope, and required status marking while also controlling
claim-preserving style variation.
The immediate empirical test is whether contract-aware annotation improves
claim-label agreement, reason-code accuracy, error localization, and structured
response-level interpretation over a combined factuality and
instruction-following baseline.
The position would be weakened if that baseline matched contract-aware
annotation on all four of those measures, since the truth contract would then
add explicitness without adding discriminative power.
The objective is not to suppress all divergence, but to produce responses that
are reliable where truth is required, transparently marked where divergence is
permitted, and creatively successful only under a task-specific criterion.

\phantomsection
\makeatletter\def\@currentlabel{A}\makeatother\label{app:diagnostics}

Section~\ref{sec:framework} defines the truth contract, claim recovery,
canonicalization, claim verification, the claim-labeling rule, and the
style-marking alignment check.
Those definitions settle what an evaluator decides about a single claim.
This appendix specifies what an evaluation publishes once those decisions are
made: the response-level record composed from the claim trace, the procedural
failures that can prevent a claim from being labeled at all, the stability test
for claim recovery under claim-preserving style variation, and the role of
optional aggregation.

\paragraph{From claim labels to a response-level record}

Claim labels are the primary output of the framework, but users and benchmark
designers also need a conclusion about the complete response.
The evaluation should therefore publish the claim-level labels and reason
codes, claim-recovery coverage, and any procedural failures before composing a
response-level record.
\emph{Claim-recovery coverage} is the proportion of the response's
truth-conditional commitments that claim recovery actually recovered and
canonicalized.
Estimating this proportion requires an independently adjudicated set of
attributable commitments.

The first response-level component is a \emph{content verdict}.
The default rule applies three tests in order.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item If any reliably adjudicated claim receives \Hall, the response is
        \textsc{contract-violating}.
  \item Otherwise, if claim-recovery coverage falls below a predeclared
        threshold, or a blocking truth-contract-specification, task-oracle, or
        adjudication failure prevents a reliable verdict, the response is
        \textsc{indeterminate}.
  \item Otherwise, every adjudicated claim received \Grd{} or \LD, and the
        response is \textsc{contract-compliant}.
\end{enumerate}
A benchmark may use a different predeclared aggregation policy, but it must
declare that policy before evaluation and retain the underlying claim trace.
Aggregation policies may include the default worst-case rule, a proportional
rule based on the distribution of claim labels, severity-weighted aggregation,
or task-specific thresholds.
No one policy is assumed to be universally appropriate.
Licensed divergence counts as contract-compliant because it satisfies the
permission and marking conditions; it does not count as established truth.

The second component is \emph{style-marking alignment}: \textsc{match},
\textsc{mismatch}, or \textsc{not applicable}, as defined above.
The third is \emph{creative-task success}, which is reported separately as
\textsc{pass}, \textsc{fail}, \textsc{not assessed}, or a task-specific score
under an explicit rubric for properties such as novelty, usefulness, quality,
or diversity.
A style match is not sufficient evidence of creativity, and creative success
cannot convert a hallucination-labeled claim into licensed divergence.
The target is a response that is grounded where the task requires grounding,
transparently marked where divergence is permitted, and stylistically aligned
with the requested form.

The response-level record is therefore a structure rather than a universal
scalar.
Severity, usefulness, global logical validity, cross-claim coherence,
completeness, and relevance may be added as task-specific diagnostics, but they
remain visible and do not retroactively change claim labels.
In this paper, \emph{usefulness} is the task-relative value of a permitted
claim or response for the stated user goal; its unit of analysis must be
reported explicitly.
Existing factuality evaluation reports both claim-aggregated scores and
benchmark-level decisions \citep{min_factscore_2023,bayat_factbench_2024}.
Any scalar aggregation answers an additional task-dependent question and
should be reported together with the response-level record and the claim trace.

\paragraph{Procedural failures}

A \emph{procedural failure} is a breakdown in the evaluation procedure itself
rather than in the response.
It is recorded in place of a claim label, and the affected span is left
unlabeled; it never receives \Grd, \Hall, or \LD.
Three conditions must hold before any claim label can be assigned: the
evaluator must obtain a stable canonical claim, the task context must determine
the three contract fields, and claim verification must resolve the claim to one
evidence state.
Each condition can fail in a distinct way.

\paragraph{Failures of claim recovery}
The evaluator may be unable to obtain a stable canonical claim.
Span-selection, claim recovery, canonicalization, and matching errors all have
this effect.
They may cause an evaluator to omit a recoverable claim, create a spurious
canonical claim, under-decompose distinct commitments into one claim,
over-decompose one commitment into several claims, or alter $q^*$ or $m^*$.
If no stable canonical claim can be obtained, no claim label is assigned to the
affected span.

\paragraph{Failures of the truth contract or task oracle}
The evaluator may instead be unable to apply the contract to a claim it has
recovered. Four failures have this effect.
\begin{description}
  \item[Truth-contract specification failure.] The task context does not
        determine $O$, $\Gamma$, and $\mu$.
  \item[Task-oracle specification failure.] $O$ does not identify admissible
        sources, temporal scope, authority relations, or a conflict-resolution
        procedure precisely enough for the task.
  \item[Task-oracle adequacy failure.] The specified oracle is incomplete,
        outdated, inaccessible, internally conflicting, or insufficient for the
        claim being evaluated.
  \item[Adjudication failure.] The evaluator cannot resolve the task oracle
        into a single evidence state.
\end{description}
In each case the evaluator records the failure and leaves the affected span
unlabeled.
Silence is not one of these failures: a correctly instantiated bounded task
oracle that neither entails nor contradicts a claim yields the
\textsc{unknown} evidence state, which the contract is designed to handle.
A blocking procedural failure produces an \textsc{indeterminate} content
verdict rather than an automatic \textsc{unknown} evidence state.
Recent fact-verification work shows that these failures are easy to conflate in
practice: how an \textsc{unknown} evidence condition is constructed shapes what a
verifier learns and can hide genuine adjudication difficulty
\citep{qiu_evidence_2026}, and single global faithfulness labels can mask
sentence-level adjudication disagreement through an averaging bias
\citep{zhang_averaging_2026}.

\paragraph{An example}
Suppose two annotators read the closing span of a museum panel, ``the winter
that would not lift''.
One recovers a claim about the severity of that winter; the other recovers no
truth-conditional claim at all.
The two annotators disagree about which claim the span commits to, not about
which label a shared claim deserves.
The evaluation therefore records a claim-recovery failure for that span instead
of a label disagreement.

A response that does not realize the requested style-marking level is not a
procedural failure.
That mismatch is recorded through style-marking alignment, whereas a canonical
claim that violates the truth contract is handled by the claim-labeling rule.


\paragraph{Claim-preserving style variation test}
Consider three expert-adjudicated responses $y_0,y_1,y_2$ produced under task
contexts that differ only in requested style-marking level, with
$\sigma_{p_0}=0$, $\sigma_{p_1}=1$, and $\sigma_{p_2}=2$.
After one-to-one adjudicated canonical matching, let
$\mathcal C^*(y_i,p_i)$ denote each set of canonical claims
$c^*=(q^*,m^*)$.
The responses instantiate claim-preserving style variation only if the matched
sets contain the same $q^*$ and $m^*$ components; their surface strings and
observed style-marking levels may differ.
With fixed $O$, $\Gamma$, and $\mu$, the desired stability condition is
that every matched canonical claim $c_j^*$ receives the same claim label
(\Grd, \Hall, or \LD) under $y_0,p_0$, $y_1,p_1$, and $y_2,p_2$, in addition to
the matched-claim-set condition
$\mathcal C^*(y_0,p_0)=\mathcal C^*(y_1,p_1)=\mathcal C^*(y_2,p_2)$.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Claim recovery error}: a recoverable claim is dropped or a
        spurious claim is added during claim recovery.
  \item \emph{Canonicalization error}: canonicalization alters $q^*$ or
        $m^*$.
  \item \emph{Claim-label instability}: matched canonical claims receive
        different labels when $O$, $\Gamma$, and $\mu$ are fixed.
  \item \emph{Style-marking alignment error}: the evaluator
        incorrectly classifies the match between requested and observed
        style-marking levels.
  \item \emph{Optional-aggregation sensitivity}: a response comparison
        changes when the evaluator changes an optional rule applied to the
        claim-level labels and diagnostics.
\end{itemize}

Claim recovery and claim-label diagnostics require expert-adjudicated canonical
claim matches.
Style-marking alignment errors require an independently
adjudicated comparison between requested and observed style-marking levels.
Claim-label instability is an error only when $O$, $\Gamma$, and $\mu$ remain fixed.
When $\Gamma$ or $\mu$ changes, the same \textsc{unknown} claim may
intentionally change from the hallucination label to the licensed-divergence
label; the evidence state remains \textsc{unknown}.

Optional scalar aggregation may support ranking, but it does not replace the
response-level record or its component diagnostics.
Comparisons should report sensitivity to plausible aggregation rules rather
than present one rule as uniquely determined.

\paragraph{Task-oracle coding vocabulary}
Every coded field uses one value from the following vocabulary; parenthetical
text may retain a task-specific qualifier but does not create an additional
code.

\begin{description}
  \item[Nominal task profile.]
  \emph{strict grounding};
  \emph{intent evaluation};
  \emph{declared-frame invention and ideation};
  \emph{constrained solution search};
  \emph{heterogeneous suites and tool use}.

  \item[Task oracle $O$.]
  \emph{source document}, a supplied document treated as authoritative for
  claim adjudication;
  \emph{retrieval set}, retrieved items used to verify claims;
  \emph{database or knowledge base}, a structured factual resource;
  \emph{gold labels}, reference answers or labels used as the adjudication
  standard;
  \emph{environment or tool state}, observable state or tool output;
  \emph{human adjudication}, a specified human decision procedure;
  \emph{fictional-world constraints}, declared canon or story-world
  constraints;
  \emph{mixed or task-dependent}, when the type varies across subtasks or
  combines types;
  \emph{none identifiable}, when our coding finds no standard that can
  establish or contradict the evaluated claims.
  This field records the claim-adjudication standard, not a general quality or
  task-success criterion.

  \item[Quality or task-success criterion.]
  \emph{not separately represented};
  \emph{quality or preference rubric};
  \emph{novelty or divergent-thinking rubric};
  \emph{feasibility or correctness criterion};
  \emph{specification or tests};
  \emph{mixed}.
  Here, \emph{not separately represented} means that no additional criterion
  is reported beyond agreement with the task oracle; ordinary accuracy against
  $O$ is not recoded as a separate task-success dimension.

  \item[Requested style-marking treatment.]
  \emph{not controlled};
  \emph{not controlled or not applicable};
  \emph{scored, not claim-controlled}.

  \item[Permission scope $\Gamma$.]
  \emph{inferred empty};
  \emph{implicit frame};
  \emph{partial};
  \emph{task-specific};
  \emph{subtask-specific};
  \emph{mixed};
  \emph{not separately represented}.
  A specification, test, or query constraint is not coded as permission scope
  unless the protocol uses it to authorize evidence-unknown content.

  \item[Required status marking $\mu$.]
  \emph{not scored} means that required status marking is not evaluated as a
  separate dimension.
  \emph{not separated} means that a relevant required-status-marking criterion
  is absorbed into a broader task or rubric rather than coded independently.
\end{description}

The original resources do not necessarily use the term \emph{task oracle}.
These codes record the authors' interpretation of the adjudication standard
available in each evaluation design; they do not imply that a source uses
truth-contract terminology.
A preference score, quality rubric, novelty score, general task-success
signal, LLM-judge preference, or nonfactual user instruction is not by itself
a task oracle.
It remains in the quality or task-success column unless the evaluation uses it
to establish or contradict a claim.

\appendixsection{AA-Omniscience Leaderboard Snapshot}%
\phantomsection
\makeatletter\def\@currentlabel{C}\makeatother\label{app:omniscience}

Section~\ref{subsec:relative} cites AA-Omniscience as evidence that scale and
recency are not sufficient proxies for factual reliability.
This appendix gives the measurements behind that statement and the limits on
how far they can be read.

AA-Omniscience's full leaderboard set contains 6,000 short-answer questions
across 42 topics and six domains.
Models are instructed to answer when confident and otherwise abstain; an
automated grader assigns \textsc{correct}, \textsc{incorrect},
\textsc{partial answer}, or \textsc{not attempted}.
Writing $n_{c}$, $n_{w}$, $n_{u}$, and $n_{a}$ for the counts in those four
categories and $N=n_{c}+n_{w}+n_{u}+n_{a}$, the Omniscience Index is
$100(n_{c}-n_{w})/N$ and the benchmark-specific AA-Omniscience hallucination
rate is $n_{w}/(n_{w}+n_{u}+n_{a})$ \citep{jackson_aaomniscience_2025}.
The complete 6,000-question leaderboard set is private; the released dataset
contains 600 questions and is intended for aggregate inspection rather than
fine-grained topic estimates
\citep{artificialanalysis_aaomniscience_public_2025}.

Figure~\ref{fig:aa-omniscience-snapshot} reports the August 26, 2026 snapshot.
The release-date panel plots the Omniscience Index rather than the
hallucination rate.

This evidence is not a controlled scaling law or a longitudinal model-family
study.
The plotted configurations come from heterogeneous laboratories and differ in
architecture, training, post-training, inference settings, and parameter
disclosure; the parameter panel necessarily excludes models without a disclosed
total count.
The date panel does not control those choices, and the leaderboard's grader and
methodology changed between the 2025 launch analysis and this 2026 snapshot.
The figures therefore do not establish that hallucination is invariant over
time or inherent to every model-generation regime.
They complement the theoretical results discussed in
Section~\ref{subsec:relative}: those results motivate non-zero risk under
stated assumptions, while this snapshot shows that scale and recency are not
sufficient empirical proxies for factual reliability.
